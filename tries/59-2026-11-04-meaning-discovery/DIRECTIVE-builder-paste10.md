## 1. Correct your understanding of the objective
The owner is not asking you to define READ, FIND, SCAN, QUERY, or any other predetermined list of words. The owner is asking you to discover a general mechanism through which the brain determines what operations constitute the contextual meaning of a word. A word can have multiple meanings. The neuron is the addressable point. Its DNA shards encode contextual operational behavior. The applicable shard is selected using the brain's actual run conditions and contextual mechanisms. The meaning is the operation or composition performed when that shard executes. Do not equate the word, point, definition, shard, and action. They are related but distinct. Not every meaning requires a direct write or physical side effect. Some meanings constrain another operation, modify its interpretation, or change its applicability. A modifier can therefore be operationally meaningful without having an independent SQL command attached to it.

## 2. The failure in the last experiment
In tries/58, you constructed 21 roots as points, quoted their definitions, bound executable spans, created inspection runs, and demonstrated that example sentences invoked those spans. That may demonstrate the execution of manually assigned root operations. It does not prove the discovery of a new meaning. The critical distinction is: manually assigned action: developer decides what a word means, writes the action, then shows the brain executing it. Discovered operational meaning: the brain examines already available grounded information and operations, derives an applicable composition, tests it, and retains the verified result. Only the second satisfies the present objective. Do not promote the existing 21-root experiment as proof of meaning discovery. Preserve it as a separate test artifact if its execution behavior is useful. Do not copy its prescribed mappings into the live brain.

## 4. The investigation you must perform
Select at least one unresolved meaning from the brain's own existing source data. Selection must be based on documented eligibility criteria, not on a prewritten answer or a convenient example supplied by the owner. For that candidate, examine the actual evidence available to Neuron: its definitions and the meanings within those definitions; relationships to already grounded meanings; applicable historical usage and contextual examples; relationships among the operations and meanings already learned; morphology and grammatical modifiers when relevant; run context and available arrival/remainder information; historical state, time, memories, or observations where relevant. Determine what those relationships imply about the candidate's operational behavior. Discover the smallest supported composition that could satisfy that interpretation. Do not invent a composition merely because it produces a plausible English explanation. If multiple compositions are possible, distinguish them through context, evidence, or controlled tests. If the evidence cannot distinguish them, record uncertainty. Do not conceal missing meaning with a fallback to a hardcoded function.

## 5. Discover the rule, not the answer
The long-term requirement is a reusable meaning-discovery mechanism. The first successful derivation is an observation, not the algorithm's proof. Examine different words, meanings, contexts, and DNA shards to discover what is common to their successful derivations. Study unsuccessful derivations as well. Determine whether the same principles explain multiple cases without prescribing each case independently. The rule may involve definitions, dependency relationships, contextual restrictions, morphological modifiers, run history, geometric relationships, or other existing Neuron constructs. You must discover which of these are necessary through evidence. Do not turn that illustrative list into a mandatory checklist, fixed weighting scheme, or new external rules engine. Do not hardcode a MEANING = ROOT_A + ROOT_B template. The appropriate formula may involve ordered operations, conditions, branching, parallel actions, state transitions, or modification of existing meanings. Its form must follow the demonstrated operational relationships.
