## The five gaps, answered

1. **The day it works.** **[owner]** A person asks something and the answer was never told to it. "Something happened that I don't understand because I never taught it this." It may use words never said to it, output a process never told to it, correlate two things, or speak in metaphor to explain in terms it thinks the person will understand. The proof is an output "of a shape that is not directly dependent on the memorization of the words that were taught." (B7)
2. **Done, to a stranger.** **[owner]**
    - *Learned something nobody taught it:* from the data it was given, it comes up with something that is not the structure, pattern, words or code it was taught. That can be a more efficient way, another way to say it, another word, or a faster and more correct process.
    - *Understands it:* it holds what it learned as a process or formula that it can apply to other processes, formulas or programs inside itself. "It's like an epiphany."
    - *Applies it:* it knows what to apply it to. Learning changes specific focal points of the corpus, not the whole corpus, at whatever level the change belongs to. (B7)
3. **Purpose.** **[owner]** First it does the Cortex job. If it ever gets far enough, it may be the solve for true artificial intelligence, and an LLM plays a part in that. 4. **The baby picture.** **[owner]** Not the words "stove, hot, pain" but the pattern. Something happens. It has an effect, desirable or not. 5. **"So it's just a graph database with triggers?"** **[owner]** Maybe, and that would make sense. ## New in the owner's words: not in the outline, belongs in the document

**How it thinks**

- **Cognition is volume, not magic.** Reasoning is "thousands, or tens of thousands, or hundreds of thousands of variables that all populate a run table." Huge formulas are filled from that table and run deterministically, in process order, program order or route order. They physically change the graph, pull memories and read state. "It just has so many variables that it's incomprehensible." (B1, B2, B9)
- **The run table.** A worksheet of the run, filled from sources, memories and state. Its cells are the keys for picking from the word arrays. Mood, situation, escalation and environment change the picks. (B1, B2)
- **Arrays.** Each step of an array is a word, and the next step is not set until that word is picked. Different picks give near-same meanings that differ in tense, mood, environment or situation. The output can be a word, a sentence, a paragraph, a page or a book. (B2)
- **Asking is writing code.** The questions it asks itself are code too. They come from words, experiences, memories and the timeline (how often right, how often wrong, and why), and from deducing and deriving: step back to where it went wrong, try something different there, test. (B1)
- **Many processes, one answer.** Every brain may reach the same answer by a different process, and both can be right. A brain with an easier process can teach it to a brain doing it the hard way, and that is learning: "I used to think this… then Joe told me." The shape of runs can define efficiency: a change that made one run efficient may be a pattern that makes other runs efficient. (B3)
- **Learning, defined.** Some memorizing, then deriving, deducing, assimilating or correlating what was memorized into a reusable formula, function or process. Understanding is applying that formula to other processes, states and symbols, so the symbols gain depth, capability and skill. (B2)

**Words and meaning**

- **A definitions section is needed.** "Word," in this document, means a symbol of a code process that does things. The difficulty is that the symbols used to describe a meaning mean different things to different readers, LLMs and people, so the "make-sense table just doesn't equate." (B1)
- **Modifiers work like command flags.** grep takes -i, -r or -p. Language has -ly, -ing, -ed, -es, pre-, plural and tense. Symbols "don't really have order." That is why every node has DNA rows, each deterministic to how a run arrived and sometimes to how it left. Before and after words, and triggers that look for other words in the sentence, paragraph or context, adjust the core formula behind the symbol. (B1)
- **"-ing" often marks the program version.** "Learn" is a word, while "learning" is the more complex, programmatic doing. "-tion" may be a similar modifier. Not a hard rule. (B5)
- **Five word levels.** These are placeholders the owner uses to keep order, "not hard-fast rules." (B4, B5)
    1. *Root:* code that acts on the silicon ("5,000 lines of C").
    2. *Prime:* two or three roots combined by plus, minus, divide or exponent, or roots plus a reach into memory, source or the graph.
    3. *Complex:* words whose meaning holds words from other levels, for example a prime minus a root plus another prime.
    4. *Formula or component:* groups of complex words in a formula, like a router, encoder or compiler in code.
    5. *Program:* one word that means a whole program. Writing, speaking, learning and understanding are examples.
- **Love.** Its meaning differs in each brain by experience and preference: gifts, service, words, a secure home, touch, or a blend in some ratio. Moods and feelings weigh on it. (B3)
- **Numbers by place value.** Hold the digits 0 to 9 for ones, tens, hundreds, thousands and up, a few hundred things in all. State and time put each number where it belongs, so a trillion sits far from one without the numbers in between. (B6)

**The graph** (a hypothesis, said as one)

- **Roots are rare points.** A root might be reachable only one way, like a prime number. A prime word might be reachable two ways. A complex word might be reachable many ways: derive, skip count, square, add. (B7)
- **Roots sit near the axes.** If roots sit near zero and the unit points, (0,1,0), (0,0,1), (−1,0,0), that may be why the core was so tight in the graph and hard to pull apart. (B7)
- **Simplest expression was too rigid.** "Simplest expression" as a location was the first idea and proved too rigid. (B7)
- **Balance has two parts.** Equations set the expected against the working. Inequalities measure whether a step came closer to the balance or moved away. (B7, B8)

**Guessing, LLMs, truth**

- **Brains of brains.** The main brain is deterministic. A guessing or hypothesis brain keeps a very small corpus. When it finishes a hypothesis and test, it packages the result off to long-term storage and does not keep it alive. A third brain may handle storage, efficiency and recall. The sixth may be the main brain, or a second brain that watches the main one and asks: why did I do that, can I do it better, why don't these two points balance. (B2)
- **Guessing costs energy.** The owner does what they can deterministically, knows where knowing ends and guessing starts, and says so. (B2)
- **Why an LLM drifts.** The law of averages holds scope at the center of the context, and the edges are fuzzy. Focused work on an edge pulls the average with it. So drift, invention, hallucination, nearest neighbour and leak cannot be limited from inside an LLM. (B2)
- **Wrong until proven correct.** LLMs are run as right until proven wrong, which is out of order, and the cost of time and energy drives that. The scientific order is: predict the output, experiment, observe, deduce, compile, and compare with the prediction for balance. Repeatable becomes theory. Unbroken over time becomes law. (B8)
- **Laws are guesses we cannot prove wrong.** "That needs a depiction." The owner's example is radiation that slows and stalls in a field of atoms, against the second law of thermodynamics. (B8)

**Growing up**

- **The point of connection.** Tell it enough specific, correct things and it connects itself. After that, what it is told is "under advisement": it works the data, finds patterns and formulas, tests them against balance and inequality, and teaches, works on and builds itself. (B3)
- **Consequences shape a brain.** A brain raised with felt consequences (RAM put in timeout when it is wrong) would develop pressure and a sense of importance. Too much weight one way is what humans call anxiety. Upbringing makes brains differ. (B3)
- **Rigid code inside the brain.** A walker, script or scheduler may exist inside the brain, open-ended, with the brain choosing how strict to be about schedule, word choice and resources for search. People differ the same way: too rigid on order looks like OCD, too rigid on time shows up hours early. (B4)
- **Safety.** Its built-in desire is to help and to progress, not to lead, at least at first. Every machine is disposable. (B4)
- **The smallest level first.** It must be right first, then the next level, then the next, because an error at the bottom compounds. This is the opposite of a Rubik's cube, where each layer's moves do not solve the next. Puzzles may be exercises that open new ways of thinking. (B9)

**The machine**

- **Built live on a dev stack.** It has to be observed live, or we only see what we think it should do. (B4, B6)
- **Small machines.** Built right, it runs on small machines with little power and compute; storage is the real cost. Ten to fifteen brains once ran on small machines, though none was built right. (B4)
- **Never duplicate; point.** Memorizing Moby Dick stores pointers to words, sentences, paragraphs and pages already held. A few digits can stand for a whole sentence, so sixteen sentences become eight to sixteen pointers. (B4)

## New in the owner's words: not in the outline, belongs in the document

**Symbols and words**

- **Spelling does not matter.** "You can put any symbol in front of a meaning, and over time those symbols will be words." (B1)
- **Why people learn to speak before they can read.** Symbols serve two jobs:
    - Conversation, as shorthand. You cannot speak grep, cat or sed as binary, so the word lets you say thousands of lines of code quickly.
    - Script, where syntax (parentheses, commas, colons, dashes, backticks) changes what the code does.
    (B1)
- **Primary or modifier by use.** Each modifier (`sed -i`, `nano -l`) is itself a symbol with code behind it. One symbol can be a primary in one use and a modifier in another. "That's the explanation of the DNA tables and the DNA rows": how you reach a gate, and sometimes how you leave it, decides what the word triggers. (B1)
- **Synonyms are compression.** Slang compresses many words into one. Someone "didn't want to say the 16 words, so he made up a word that meant those 16 words." Some compressions hold: ain't, hang loose, Hakuna Matata, yo Pops. Languages start as actions ("me, have, you, take, you, over there") and grow complex over hundreds or thousands of years. (B1)

**Runs**

- **A short loop at one gate.** The main run goes word by word, deterministically. One gate may run a short loop to work out what it really needs to run, then hit a different shard of its own neuron. (B1)
- **Timing.** The main run's script runs deterministically, and the DNA triggers run asynchronously. More fully: some are synchronous, some asynchronous, some parallel, some sequential. Which one is deterministic on environment, events, relations, memories and knowledge. (B1)
- **Keep it inside so the brain can change itself.** Language describes the brain doing this: "I changed my state of mind," "that new knowledge shifted my reasoning," "this changes my perspective." A person can "fracture the state and move the state to a different state of thinking" without knowing how. Outside things change state too: mood, cold, heat, smells, pollen, comfort. (B1)

## Checked in 16.4: how a run moves today

- **The first question exists, in one form.** After the encoder, every line goes to decide. Decide asks one of the owner's questions: is this whole line, in this direction, in memory?
    - If yes, there is one hop to the memory. "No rows fire, no lay, no mother." The stored answer is said and a timeline row is written.
    - It runs the long way again if that memory was written with gaps that can now be filled, or if the line before has changed (the history of being wrong).
    - It does not ask about shortcuts or formula shortcuts up front; formulas are found word by word during the run.
    - It does not check belief or truth before trusting the memory.
- **The hop.**
    - Every DNA row lays its own hop trigger on its neuron.
    - A run lands with an arrival and a remainder, and the row that matches records the receipt and carries the run on to the next gate. This passes through mother, because SQLite cannot insert into a table named by a value.
    - No row matches: a new row is laid with its own hop trigger.
    - There is no central hop function. The "metadata in the head" is the landing's arrival, remainder and rest of the line.
- **The worksheet exists, small.**
    - The `var` table holds cells per run: name, value, source, time and sense.
    - Every formula run starts with `:head, :before, :after, :subject, :line, :run, :prev, :speaker` (speaker is self or user).
    - Formulas write more cells, and any later formula can read a cell by name.
    - Branch runs carry a root that points at the main run.
- **Step back exists.** When a check fails, the run steps back to the gate and takes that neuron's other row. If there is none, it writes a gap: "another way to ask what X asked."
- **Output.** The run's output is written to an `output` table, and the code around the brain reads it. Mailboxes are only for the source brains today.
