Abstract
Neuron proposes that a machine's acquired knowledge, memory, and executable behavior can 
inhabit a common mathematical structure. A neuron is conceived as a location identified by an 
expression. Its local relationships determine how an arriving run continues. Runs compose those 
relationships into processes; receipts preserve the processes actually taken; memories connect their 
content and outcomes to occurrences in time. Learning changes the structure through which later 
activity proceeds. The central architectural claim is that the graph itself can constitute the machine's 
code, including progressively more of the code by which the machine learns.
The project begins with a distinction between developing a capability and using a capability already 
developed. Expensive discovery should leave behind reusable structure. Familiar work can then 
proceed through established relationships, substitutions, and shortcuts. When a remembered 
process does not quite fit, its retained derivation can be reopened: the system can move backward to 
a relevant choice, examine applicable local alternatives, and construct a different continuation. A 
memory is consequently more than a stored answer. It is potential material for a new act of 
reasoning.
Language supplies an early interface to this architecture. Words are external forms associated with 
internal locations; their meanings are constrained by relationships, definitions, experience, and 
context. A location can be partially understood before its name is known. A sentence can lead to an 
answer, an information-gathering process, an action, or a question. The same structure is intended 
eventually to support tools, compilation, self-revision, and internally initiated activity. Later 
proposals add selective consolidation during idle periods and small local predictive models whose 
outputs remain explicitly marked as guesses.
This thesis develops the conceptual system and its research implications from the owner's prose. It 
also examines the boundary between the intended architecture and the recorded implementation. 
The archive contains bounded demonstrations, supplied procedures, failed interpretations, 
incomplete integrations, and unresolved hypotheses. It does not establish a completed autonomous 
cognitive organism, equivalence to a human brain, or the predicted performance at enormous scale. 
The resulting account is a thesis of a research program: intelligence is proposed as the continuing 
conversion of situated experience into executable, revisable structure.

Reading this manuscript
The chapters present one connected account of Neuron, moving from its purpose through its 
mechanisms to its proposed developmental life. They are written primarily in paragraphs so the 
system can be read as an argument rather than as a software checklist. The final chapters assess the 
experimental record and explain what would constitute a convincing realization of the thesis. A 
reference lexicon and source register follow the main text.
The account uses the project owner's explanations as the principal authority for intended behavior. 
Assistant interpretations are useful secondary material, especially where they record a correction or 
an experiment, but their presence in Cortex does not turn them into the owner's claims. Likewise, a 
stored report of a successful run is evidence that the report exists; it is not a fresh reproduction of 
that run.
The source corpus for this edition concentrates on the neuron-v2 book, which contains the V16 and 
V16.2 discussions despite its name. The principal material dates from September 24 through October 
11, 2026. Earlier design is discussed where it is explicitly carried into those records. The account 
does not claim to exhaust every conversation in Cortex. It brings together the substantial owner 
prose recovered for this task, including the major later corrections and extensions, rather than 
presenting a search result as an exhaustive archive.
Bracketed source labels refer to the register at the end. The accompanying sources folder preserves 
the retrieved Cortex text with its location identifiers and continuation markers. These are retrieval 
copies, not substitutes for the immutable originals or their recorded hashes. No developmental 
experiment was run to produce this manuscript.

1. The problem Neuron is trying to solve
The immediate problem behind Neuron is continuity. The owner describes a recurring experience of 
developing increasingly complicated systems with language-model assistants: a worker can perform 
a local task while losing the larger reason for that task. It can repeat the project description 
accurately and still make a decision inconsistent with it. A new component may function in isolation 
while moving the whole project away from its intended form. As the scope grows, the owner must 
repeatedly reconstruct that form for the workers. The effort of maintaining the project begins to 
compete with the effort of building it.
Cortex addresses part of this problem by preserving project evidence outside a single conversation. It 
supplies a place to recover intent, previous attempts, corrections, observations, and unfinished work. 
Yet the owner's experience distinguishes preservation from effective use. The existence of a record 
does not ensure that a worker retrieves it, interprets it correctly, or allows it to constrain the next 
action. A memory system can retain a design that its users repeatedly violate. The practical question 
therefore becomes how to make past experience operative in future behavior.
Neuron approaches that question at the level of the machine's organization. What would it mean for a 
system's experience to become part of the structure that actually selects and performs its actions? 
What would it mean for a correction to change the next relevant decision, rather than simply add 
another paragraph to an archive? The proposed answer is to make acquired relationships, 
remembered processes, and executable continuations belong to the same developing structure.
The desired system should recognize a familiar problem, retrieve relevant experience, assess 
whether an old process applies, and use or revise that process. It should preserve where its 
knowledge came from and where its uncertainty begins. When it discovers that an earlier approach 
failed, the failure should become a usable constraint on later investigation. When it encounters a 
related problem, it should carry forward the work that remains valid. These are demands for 
cumulative capability.
Conversation is an important early demonstration because language exposes many of these demands 
at once. An apparently simple question requires identifying what is being asked, distinguishing 
remembered information from missing information, selecting a response, and often deciding 
whether a question should be asked in return. The owner's later shopping and diagnostic examples 
make clear that fluent wording alone is insufficient. The machine must connect an utterance to a 
useful process under the actual circumstances.
The long-term aspiration extends beyond conversation. The owner imagines a substrate able to 
support tools, memories, compilers, logical operations, and the procedures by which further 
capability is acquired. Neuron is intended to become part of Cortex and might eventually replace 
much of its machinery, depending on what the experiment achieves. Cortex is presently the evidence 
and continuity system for the project; Neuron is the proposed computational organism being 
developed within that wider undertaking.
The personal origin of the theory matters without becoming a scientific proof. The owner repeatedly 
explains the proposal through accounts of remembering, diagnosing, deciding, and revising. The

2. The graph as a computational medium
The central statement of Neuron is that the graph is the code. To understand the statement, it is 
necessary to take seriously the distinction between storing a description of a process and embodying 
the process in executable structure. A conventional-looking graph can store the fact that two things 
are related while an outside program decides how to interpret that relationship. Neuron's intended 
graph contains the relationships, conditions, transitions, and continuations through which the work 
itself happens.
A location is therefore potentially an entry into behavior. What that behavior accomplishes depends 
on the surrounding structure and on how a run arrives. A sequence of local transitions can read, 
compare, select, carry a result forward, or enter another process. A larger structure can combine 
these operations. In the owner's account, there is no categorical division by which a neuron may 
represent a word but cannot represent a compiler, a memory operation, or a tool. Each must be 
realized as an appropriate arrangement of the same computational medium.
This does not mean that naming a location after an operation makes the operation exist. A location 
called search acquires no searching ability from its label. The structure reached from that location 
must perform the relevant matching, progression, selection, and return. The owner's repeated 
objection to external Python helpers is directed at precisely this gap. A graph can appear to perform a 
task while an outside routine supplies the decisive work. Such a demonstration may produce the 
desired output while failing to investigate the proposed architecture.
The objection is ultimately about ownership of behavior, rather than the spelling of a programming 
language. Later owner prose explicitly extends it to shell scripts. Replacing a Python helper with a 
shell helper does not transfer the capability into Neuron. Conversely, the existence of an external 
workbench used by researchers to inspect evidence or prepare a lesson does not establish that the 
brain itself depends on that workbench for the cognitive operation being claimed. The boundary 
must be understood in terms of what chooses and performs the work.
The project acknowledges a developmental difficulty here. A young structure cannot already contain 
every capability required to construct its mature form. Early support may supply teaching, storage 
access, and execution machinery. The intended direction is for learned or taught graph procedures to 
assume increasing responsibility. The owner's stronger hypothesis is that much of what initially 
appears to require external structural machinery may later be expressed through Neuron's own 
code. That is a bootstrapping ambition whose extent must be demonstrated.
A useful way to state the thesis is that the machine's operative organization should be inspectable in 
the graph. If a decision changes because the system learned something, the relevant changed 
relationship, procedure, or selection condition should exist there. If a procedure is reused, the

through arrangements that also determine what it can do. [S02, S05, S07, S20, S23]
3. Mathematical identity, location, and relationship
Neuron assigns a foundational role to mathematical identity. The early registration contract 
separates the identity of a result from the identifier used to store it. A database row number is a 
bookkeeping fact. A mathematical expression is intended to identify the location itself. This 
distinction prevents the architecture from becoming a collection of arbitrary identifiers with 
mathematical descriptions attached afterward.
The original example concerns a fraction. A result such as one half can exist as a terminal location 
even when no further applicable relationship is known. Later learning may establish that several 
expressions arrive at that same mathematical result. The expressions that produced it remain 
significant because they preserve different derivations. Convergence therefore does not require 
erasing the routes by which convergence was reached.
The later V16 prose develops the idea into a space of expression locations. Expressions such as X, a 
reciprocal of X, or an offset involving another variable illustrate distinct structural identities. Their 
role is not necessarily to demand numerical evaluation during every act of use. An expression can 
identify a destination and the relations by which it is reached. The graph becomes the materialized 
part of a mathematical organization that the learner has so far established.
Relationships constrain where a new location belongs. The owner's recurring image is triangulation: 
one known relationship provides a partial constraint; additional relationships restrict the 
possibilities further. A concept that participates in several families must fit the combined structure. 
Learning more about a concept can therefore refine its placement. An early placement may be useful 
and provisional, while later evidence requires movement or a change in the relation that placed it.
The word triangulation is an analogy for converging constraints. It should not be read as a 
declaration that all meanings occupy literal points in ordinary three-dimensional space. The owner 
also describes many planes and levels, using spatial imagery to communicate interconnection. The 
research obligation is to determine what the dimensions and relations mean in the actual 
representation. A drawing in three dimensions would not by itself establish the semantic adequacy of 
that representation.
The most important interpretive correction is that the owner's equations are pseudocode unless 
explicitly established otherwise. A reciprocal used to illustrate an opposition does not commit the 
system to reciprocals as the universal mathematics of opposition. A power used to illustrate

This distinction preserves both the clarity and the openness of the project. The owner is asserting

Mathematical convergence also creates a practical question. Two routes may legitimately reach the 
same identity, but accidental collisions can merge things that need to remain distinct. The archive

informative because they expose the difference between obtaining a consistent expression and 
obtaining a useful cognitive distinction. A mature system must preserve the distinctions required by 
its learned relationships and contexts.
The thesis thus combines exactness with revisability. A realized location should have a definite 
identity under the representation in use. The interpretation and placement that produced it can still 
be corrected. Exact structure is not a declaration of infallible knowledge; it is what makes the 
consequences of a proposed understanding concrete enough to inspect, reuse, and revise. [S01, S02,

4. DNA, arrival, and the structure of a run
The project's term DNA refers to local executable relationships and continuations associated with a 
neuron. It is an architectural metaphor, not a claim that the machine reproduces molecular biology. A 
shard of DNA records how a relevant arrival can proceed: what relationship or operation applies, 
where the next step leads, and what work remains. The neuron is a location; its local structure makes 
that location participate in different processes.
Arrival is essential because a destination alone does not tell the whole story of a computation. Two 
runs can reach the same location with different histories, bindings, questions, or unfinished work. 
Their next useful steps may differ. The owner's examples repeatedly insist that how a run arrived 
helps determine which shard applies. The location is therefore not a context-free instruction that 
always emits the same next location.
A run is the organized activity that follows these relationships. At a simple level, it is a sequence of 
transitions. At a more developed level, it may enter a subrun, carry a result back, follow a conditional 
alternative, or compose several subordinate processes. The owner's later formulation makes the 
identification explicit: the runs are the formulas. A complicated formula can be a run whose parts are 
themselves formulas, including formulas that select which other formulas to use.
This gives a precise role to remaining work. A process can arrive at a point with one part completed 
and another part still unresolved. The continuation must preserve enough context to distinguish that 
state from other uses of the same point. The architecture must also preserve bindings when several

branches use the same value or when an intermediate result becomes an input to a larger process. 
These requirements are consequences of the owner's process examples, even where the final 
implementation schema remains unsettled.
The original walkthrough of memory formation illustrates this continuity. An input traverses several 
gates through selected DNA. A receipt represents the route. The process then continues into memory, 
takes or associates a clock snapshot, records an occurrence on the timeline, and reaches output. The 
owner describes output as a mailbox observed by the decoder. The whole sequence is intended as 
work expressed through the brain, rather than a collection of after-the-fact operations secretly 
coordinated outside it.
It is useful to distinguish three things within this account. The location identifies where the activity is. 
The applicable DNA identifies how activity can proceed there. The receipt identifies what actually 
happened in a particular realized process. These distinctions permit a common destination to 
participate in several derivations without losing their differences. They also make later explanation 
and adaptation possible.
The exact definition of an arrival key, a receipt encoding, or a storage row is an implementation 
matter that changed during the project. This manuscript does not promote an early schema into 
permanent law. The stable principle is that executable continuations must be sensitive to the 
distinctions required by their processes, and the system must retain enough of the actual route to 
recover those distinctions later.
A neuronal run is therefore both an act and a potential object of future thought. It performs a process 
now, while leaving a structure that can later be recalled, compared, shortened, reopened, or revised. 
That double role is the connection between execution and experience on which much of the rest of 
the system depends. [S02, S04, S09, S11]
5. Birth, propagation, and the edge of knowledge
The system's development begins at the boundary of what has already been realized. A mathematical 
location can exist without a known continuation. The absence of further knowledge does not make 
the location invalid; it establishes the current limit of what the structure can do there. Learning 
changes that limit by supplying or discovering another applicable relationship.
Birth has more than one context in the records. A newly established mathematical result may require 
a new location. A completed receipt may require a memory location if that receipt is not already 
represented. A new occurrence of an existing process may require another historical record without 
requiring a new identity for the shared process. These should not be collapsed into a single 
undifferentiated instruction to create a neuron whenever something happens.
The owner's initial contract emphasizes propagation. A new mathematical capability may apply to 
previously realized structures. Applying it can produce new results, and those results can make older 
capabilities newly applicable. Development may consequently proceed through a succession of local 
consequences. A new lesson need not remain attached only to the one example through which it was 
introduced.

The later term back-population describes this application of newly available relationships to relevant 
existing structure. The important word is relevant. The owner repeatedly rejects a routine that 
applies every learned formula to every labeled gate. A relation about memory need not apply to 
every word; a relation about a class of forms need not apply to every process. Applicability is itself a 
substantive requirement, not an inconvenience to remove by scanning everything.
This creates a developmental picture in which knowledge grows along established connections. A 
new word should be taught through relations to what the system already knows. Where several 
relations converge, the placement becomes more constrained. The owner asks for learning at the 
edge because the edge provides contact between the unknown and the existing structure. Simply 
importing a vocabulary into arbitrary disconnected locations would not investigate that method of 
growth.
The archive also exposes a real limit to the idea. Mathematical possibility can be enormous or 
unbounded. A rule that applies again to its own output may generate an indefinitely extensible 
sequence. The original statement that propagation stops where learned mathematics no longer 
applies describes a desired natural boundary, but it is not a general termination theorem. The V16 
discussions explicitly acknowledge unresolved fan-out: when should a mathematically possible 
destination be realized, and when should it remain a possibility?
The correct response is to retain that question within the thesis. Selectivity, applicability, evidence, 
and resource limits all matter, but a candidate policy must be identified as a policy rather than 
quietly attributed to the owner. The project's claim is that useful development can exploit the 
consequences of acquired structure. It does not establish that unconstrained closure over all 
conceivable relationships is desirable or feasible.
The edge of knowledge is also an epistemic boundary. A location can have a partial definition, 
uncertain placement, or a missing relation. Its incomplete state should remain available as such. The 
system is meant to grow through those incompletions, not conceal them with fabricated names or 
final answers. A well-represented unknown is already useful structure because it tells later learning 
where new evidence may matter. [S01, S02, S03, S06, S12]
6. Learning, execution, and the meaning of a shortcut
The owner's distinction between learning and execution is one of the project's organizing ideas. 
Learning performs the work required to establish a useful process. Execution uses the process once it 
exists. If every later use must rediscover the relationships that made the first use possible, the system 
has failed to capture much of the value of learning.
This distinction is expressed in the archive through long and short forms of a run. The long form 
exposes the internal steps by which a result is reached. A shortcut permits established work to be 
reused at a higher level. An input can be bound to a known process, a known relation can be 
followed, and a previously established subresult can be used without rederiving its justification on 
every occasion.

There are several kinds of reuse within this idea. Recalling an answer to the same stable question is 
one. Applying an established relationship to a suitable input is another. Executing a parameterized 
process with changed bindings is a third. They offer different savings and support different claims. A 
system that remembers a particular result has not thereby demonstrated a general procedure for all 
new inputs; a system that applies a supplied procedure has not thereby discovered that procedure.
The owner sometimes describes ordinary use as traversal without computation, or with very little 
computation. The strongest coherent reading is that familiar use avoids repeating the expensive 
work of discovery. Traversal, substitution, comparison, and memory access still perform work. The 
architectural hypothesis concerns how much work can be prepared and reused, and how little of the 
original derivation needs to be repeated. It should not be weakened into a claim that all computation 
vanishes.
The illustrative reduction of a very deep run to a small number of formula-level hops communicates 
this ambition. It is not a measured universal speedup. Whether a shortcut is useful depends on what 
it preserves, how its inputs are bound, whether the needed result is already available, and how 
expensive its supporting representation is. The thesis predicts opportunities for cheap familiar use; 
the actual scale of those opportunities requires measurement.
The long form remains essential because reuse is conditional. A process may have depended on an 
assumption that is now false, on evidence that has been withdrawn, or on a distinction that was 
previously ignored. If the shortcut becomes an opaque answer with no recoverable support, the 
system loses the material it needs for correction. The owner therefore retains both the established 
route and the ability to reopen it.
A shortcut's receipt must also represent the work that actually occurred. If a run used a stored result 
or a shorter process, its record should not falsely claim that the full derivation happened again. The 
supporting long route can remain linked as the basis for reuse. This preserves the distinction 
between present execution and historical justification.
The more ambitious form of reuse is developmental. A similar problem can be approached through 
an old process, with a useful internal choice changed and the remaining structure retained. This is 
not merely faster execution; it is a proposal for cheaper learning. The next new process begins from 
the organized consequences of prior experience. Neuron's shortcuts therefore serve both immediate 
efficiency and cumulative invention. [S05, S07, S08, S09, S11]
7. Receipts, memories, and the continuity of 
experience
A run receipt is the system's record of a realized process. In the owner's conception it is central to 
memory identity: the run becomes an expression or structured identity at which the experience can 
be retained. The receipt is not merely a human-readable log message stating that something 
succeeded. It represents the route through which something happened.

The original walkthrough connects a completed run to a memory neuron. If the corresponding 
memory identity is absent, it is created; if it is already present, a further occurrence can be associated 
with it. The process then enters the timeline with a clock-related record and continues toward 
output. The arithmetic used to illustrate this mechanism is pseudocode, but the functional 
distinctions are clear: content can recur, occurrences differ, and historical placement must remain 
recoverable.
A useful memory includes more than its final answer. To support the owner's proposed reasoning, it 
must make relevant inputs, conditions, choices, outcomes, and sources accessible. The system needs 
to ask not only whether something happened before, but what was done, whether it worked, and 
why the earlier case might apply now. If all that survives is the final output, much of the experience 
has been discarded.
The concept also broadens memory beyond autobiography. A dictionary entry can be a run whose 
receipt preserves the definition. A tool can be represented by the structure of its executable process. 
A learned relation can have a history of supporting and contradicting observations. In this 
architecture, knowledge and memory overlap because both are available through realized structure. 
Their roles still differ: a general procedure is not identical to every occasion on which it was used.
That distinction becomes especially important for repetition. A process may remain the same across 
many occurrences while the speaker, time, expectation, or outcome changes. Sharing the process 
does not justify merging those events into one event. Conversely, preserving distinct events does not 
require copying the entire procedure into each occurrence. The conceptual model makes room for 
shared structure and differentiated history.
Memory also carries the system's failures. A trial that did not work is valuable if the conditions and 
reason for failure remain accessible. The owner's recurring question is whether something now 
known might change the significance of an earlier failed attempt. This makes failed experience a 
potential starting point for later learning. The system should neither repeat it blindly nor exclude it 
forever without considering changed conditions.
A receipt can become an explanation when it supports a faithful account of how a result was reached. 
Yet explanation has two forms here. One traces the actual present run. Another explains the 
historical discovery or evidence that made an available shortcut trustworthy. Both may matter, and 
the system should be able to distinguish them. A shortcut can have a short execution history and a 
substantial supporting history.
The continuity Neuron seeks is consequently active continuity. Past experience remains connected to 
present choice through reusable, inspectable, revisable structure. A memory is something the 
machine can do work with: enter, compare, adapt, challenge, and use to select a next action. The 
project succeeds at this level only when the preserved experience changes later behavior in the 
intended way. [S04, S06, S07, S09, S16, S18]

8. Time, state, and the difference between knowing 
and having known
Time gives the system a way to distinguish an experience from its later interpretation. A relation may 
have been accepted yesterday and questioned today. A prediction may have been made before its 
outcome was available. A process may have failed under conditions that subsequently changed. 
Without temporal structure, these differences collapse into a collection of statements whose order 
and significance are difficult to recover.
The owner's emphasis on timeline is therefore more substantial than a demand for timestamps. A 
clock can label an event, but a useful history must connect the event to the state in which it occurred. 
What did the system know then? Which assumptions did it use? What was it trying to accomplish? 
What evidence became available afterward? These questions make time part of reasoning.
Consider the distinction between hearing a claim and observing the event described by the claim. In 
the owner's example, another person reports that a cat sat down. The listener may judge the 
statement plausible from previous experience and may have reasons to trust the speaker. Those are 
real grounds for a provisional belief. They are not the same event as directly observing the cat sit. 
The system must retain the source of its information rather than flatten all routes into a single 
undifferentiated fact.
The example is a conceptual model of evidence, not a claim that direct observation is infallible. Its 
importance is that different grounds can support different judgments and later revisions. If the 
report is contradicted, the system can investigate the report, its interpretation, or the speaker's 
reliability without erasing the fact that the report was received. What happened to the machine and 
what happened in the world are related records, not automatically identical records.
State also includes the conditions that make a familiar procedure applicable. An instruction can be 
appropriate under one goal and inappropriate under another. A remembered answer can be accurate 
for an earlier time and stale now. An earlier failure can be decisive under unchanged assumptions but 
less informative after a new capability has been acquired. Neuron is intended to use these 
distinctions when deciding whether to reuse or reopen a process.
A particularly important temporal relation connects a guess to its later evaluation. The guess must be 
preserved as a guess made with the evidence available at the time. A later confirmation should not 
rewrite the original event as certain knowledge. A later refutation should not erase the fact that the 
system had a reason to investigate it. The historical sequence explains how the machine learned.
This leads to a model of correction that preserves rather than destroys continuity. The old 
representation remains part of the system's history, while the operative structure changes. A new 
interpretation of an old event is itself a new event. The system can then distinguish what was 
believed, what changed, and what consequences followed from the change.
The archive does not supply a completed solution to storing every relevant state economically. A full 
snapshot of everything at every moment would be a different and much more expensive commitment 
than retaining the dependencies necessary to interpret an occurrence. The thesis establishes the

them without treating every event as a complete copy of the brain. [S04, S06, S08, S11, S18]
9. Meaning before a name
One of Neuron's most distinctive proposals is that the system can know something about a concept 
before knowing its word. Relationships may constrain a location whose external form has not been 
taught. The machine may know how that location differs from a known concept, resembles another, 
or participates in a familiar structure. A missing label is not necessarily a total absence of meaning.
The owner's challenge involving the word little clarifies this idea. A system was reported as 
producing the expected word after an inference. The owner asked how the decoder knew that word if 
the concept was supposedly unknown. If the label had already been supplied through teaching or 
encoding, printing it could not establish that the system had discovered a previously unknown word. 
The experiment had to distinguish relation inference, concept formation, and naming.
The intended alternative is more informative. The system can report that it does not know the word 
while describing the relationships that constrain the unnamed location. It can state a partial 
definition, identify what is inferred, and ask whether a proposed name or correction is appropriate. 
Success would lie in the supported structure of the description, not in the accidental production of a 
familiar string.
This makes definitions part of the working architecture. The owner rejects a bare grammatical label 
as an adequate definition. A full entry should contain enough information to distinguish senses, uses, 
relationships, and examples. The exact schema may vary by language and by the kind of thing being 
defined. The definition is itself represented as a run, and its receipt becomes accessible from the 
location.
The later requirement for an updatable Relationships section develops the idea further. As 
relationships reach a point, the definition should gain usable information about them. A definition is 
consequently not a fixed paragraph inserted once and forgotten. It is a growing account of what the 
system can currently establish about that location, with revisions that can be connected to their 
sources and circumstances.
This creates a reciprocal relation between definition and placement. Relationships help place a 
concept, while a definition helps determine whether a proposed placement makes sense. If a new 
mathematical route leads to a location whose known definition is incompatible with the proposed 
interpretation, that mismatch is evidence worth investigating. The system may have selected the 
wrong sense, proposed an unsuitable formula, or discovered that an earlier placement was 
inadequate.
Meaning must also remain sensitive to context. Several uses of one written word may require distinct 
internal treatment. Several external forms may express closely related or overlapping meanings. The 
archive's candidate mappings do not establish a universal rule that every spelling corresponds to one 
indivisible meaning. The demand for full definitions and sense-specific relationships points toward a 
richer organization.

10. Language as organized activity
Language enters Neuron through an encoder and leaves through a decoder. The owner's central 
boundary is that external words are forms associated with internal expression locations. The 
cognitive graph should not depend on English labels as the identities of its operations. A readable 
name helps humans communicate with and inspect the machine, but the internal work must follow 
the relationships represented there.
The encoder is also conceived as a sensor. It brings information about the occurrence of an input: 
who supplied it, when it arrived, and through what channel or circumstances it was obtained. That 
surrounding information matters because the same string can have different significance depending 
on its source, time, and purpose. Language without such context cannot carry the full situated 
meaning the owner intends.
The project treats the components of written expression broadly. Punctuation, grouping symbols, 
spacing or formatting actions where relevant, and special keys such as Enter are to have 
representations in the neuronal medium. The owner's objection to a tokenizer is a rejection of using 
an external semantic preparation stage to perform work attributed to the brain. It is an architectural 
requirement about how forms are represented and used, rather than a demonstrated solution to all 
segmentation problems.
A sentence then becomes an organized run through locations whose roles affect its composition. The 
distinction between a statement and a question cannot be reduced to noticing that both contain the 
same words. Their conditions of use and required continuations differ. A question may direct the 
system toward a missing value, a verification, a procedure, or a request for evidence. Grammar and 
punctuation help express these differences, but the actual operators remain to be discovered or 
taught.
The owner's illustrative equations for punctuation should be read at this level. An exponent can 
stand in the explanation for a transformation of an entire utterance. It is not a claim that exclamation 
literally means exponentiation. The research question concerns how an operation changes the 
interpretation and continuation of a run. Several kinds of mathematical structure may be needed to 
express those changes.
Tense adds another layer. A statement about what happened, a question about what is happening, 
and a plan for what should happen invoke different relations to time and evidence. A useful 
representation must preserve the event being discussed and its temporal role. Simply attaching a 
past-tense label to a present claim would not establish the required relation to remembered 
occurrences.

The later owner discussion of keywords provides a more focused hypothesis about interpretation. 
Words such as how, know, decide, and memory can help identify the kind of work requested and the

interpretation without requiring a search through every available reasoning procedure.
The proposal is not that one surface word always selects one fixed operation. Context and

whereas a question about how something could be done may require constructing a procedure. The 
same visible cue can participate in different tasks. The owner explicitly returns to subject, predicate, 
context, and combinations of words when refining this hypothesis.
Language is thus treated as an entry into action. Understanding a sentence means identifying what 
process it calls for under the circumstances. The eventual output may be another sentence, but it can 
also be an investigation, a tool use, a plan, or a question that resolves a missing dependency. A 
mature answer is the result of organized activity, and the language used to express it should remain 
connected to that activity. [S02, S04, S07, S09, S11, S20]
11. Reasoning as the composition of processes
The owner's account of reasoning begins from a practical observation: a person does not usually 
approach a familiar problem by testing every possible explanation in an arbitrary order. Experience 
suggests what to examine first. A pattern in the present situation recalls an earlier case. That case 
supplies a possible process, a remembered outcome, and a set of questions whose answers may 
determine whether the process applies.
The air-conditioning example is especially useful because it makes reasoning concrete. The owner 
describes recognizing features of a malfunction, recalling comparable cases, selecting a relevant 
observation or measurement, and using the result to narrow or change the investigation. The value of 
the example lies in the organization of inquiry. It is not presented here as a repair procedure or as 
validation of the transcript's informal electrical arithmetic.
A larger problem can contain many unknowns, but they need not all be resolved at once. Some are 
accessible through observations. Others can be inferred once their dependencies are available. A 
result may become the input to another process. A surprising result may change the question 
entirely. The investigator can move from asking why a system does not work to asking why a 
particular component or relation behaves unexpectedly.
Neuron expresses this organization through formulas composed of other formulas. A parent process 
depends on subordinate results. Those results may require their own processes, and the choice of 
subordinate processes can itself be computed from context. The repeated word formula in the 
owner's prose covers several roles: a relation, a procedure, a test, a selection rule, and a composition 
of those elements. Later vocabulary work attempts to separate such roles without losing their 
common representation.

The analogy to an equation is useful because it identifies dependencies. A known relation plus 
sufficient known variables can determine a missing variable. More generally, knowing what would 
resolve the problem tells the system what it needs to establish next. Yet the proposed reasoning 
process is broader than substituting numbers into a fixed equation. It includes discovering which 
relation applies, deciding which variable can be obtained, and changing the frame when the evidence 
no longer fits.
The owner also emphasizes that a reasoning process can have a different shape in different cases. A 
short case may need only a few established steps. A difficult case may need several kinds of 
reasoning, nested investigations, or a comparison among alternative models. There is no 
requirement that every question traverse a universal fixed sequence. The content of the problem 
helps select the relevant composition.
Analogy is important because it supplies a starting structure across domains. The owner uses a 
familiar diagnostic process to explain a less familiar computational problem. The shared feature is a 
pattern of dependencies and tests, not the physical identity of the objects. A useful machine analogue 
would preserve the relation that transfers while checking the parts that do not.
This model also explains why reasoning and learning overlap. If the system already has a suitable 
process and all required information, it can execute. If some necessary relation or value is missing, it 
must investigate. A successful investigation can leave behind a new process or a revised relation. The 
answer to the present question becomes part of what makes the next related question easier.
The proposed unity is not established by declaring every mental activity to be a formula. It becomes 
meaningful when particular processes can be represented, selected, executed, compared with 
outcomes, and revised. The owner's prose supplies a coherent pattern for those operations. The 
research task is to show that the pattern can develop into useful, transferable behavior within 
Neuron itself. [S06, S08, S09, S20, S25]
12. Questions as instruments of thought
A question is an operation aimed at a gap. Its purpose is not exhausted by its grammar. It asks for 
information that would resolve a dependency, discriminate between alternatives, establish a cause, 
recover a method, or determine whether the system should act. In Neuron, the ability to choose a 
useful question is therefore part of the cognitive capability being sought.
The owner repeatedly asks how the brain knows what to ask. This is a deeper problem than 
generating a well-formed interrogative sentence. A machine can ask a fluent question that does not 
help. It can also possess a relevant fact while failing to recognize that the fact answers the current 
uncertainty. Question selection connects the present problem to the structure of knowledge and the 
means of obtaining more.
The diagnostic example illustrates the connection. A process requires an unknown property. The 
system may know an observation that would reveal it, or a relation that would infer it from other 
available properties. The useful next question depends on which route is available and how much the

Neuron thesis  ·  18
answer would change the investigation. In the owner's terms, there are formulas for obtaining 
variables and formulas for deciding which variables matter next.
The archive contains assistant hypotheses that assign special roles to question words, including 
distinctions between missing causes and missing routes. Those proposals are interpretations of 
particular monologues. They should not be elevated into a final psychology of why and how. The later 
owner prose adds more cues and emphasizes context, historical knowledge, and the task implied by a 
sentence.
An operational account should therefore distinguish the source of a question from its wording. A 
discrepancy between expected and observed outcomes may produce a causal question. A known goal 
with no known route may produce a procedural question. An uncertain report may produce a 
request for evidence. A conflict between two plausible interpretations may produce a discriminating 
question. These are explanatory categories for the thesis, not a claim that the archive implemented a 
complete classifier for them.
Questions can also address the system's own history. Have I tried this? Under what conditions? What 
happened? What did I know at the time? What has changed since? Such questions make memory 
useful to reasoning. Without them, the archive is merely available; with them, prior experience 
becomes a directed resource.
The owner expects the developing brain eventually to return questions that were not explicitly 
taught as the answers to particular prompts. A teacher should be able to see why the question 
follows from the student's state. This would provide evidence that the system is selecting its next 
information need, rather than reciting a lesson. The distinction is especially important when the 
teacher is itself capable of supplying sophisticated reasoning.
A question can thus be both an output and a continuation. It suspends one process while seeking 
what that process needs. The returned information must then enter the original context, alter the 
relevant state, and allow work to continue. Producing the question without this return path would 
leave the developmental loop incomplete.
The mature proposal treats inquiry as reusable structure. A successful way of resolving a kind of 
uncertainty becomes available for later cases. A poorly chosen question leaves evidence about why it 
failed to discriminate or advance the task. The system learns not only answers, but ways of becoming 
less ignorant about the questions it cannot yet answer. [S06, S08, S11, S20, S21]
13. Recursive adaptation and the recovery of 
alternatives
The owner's explanation of recursive shortcuts extends reuse into a method of invention. A 
remembered process may be close to what the present problem needs without being exactly suitable. 
Instead of beginning from nothing, the system can start with the old outcome and move backward 
through the receipt to a point where a different continuation might help.

Neuron thesis  ·  19
At that point, the search is local. The owner explicitly rejects stepping backward once and then 
searching the entire corpus, repeating the same expensive search at each earlier step. The relevant 
alternatives are found in the structure associated with the reached location. More specifically, the 
October 1 explanation limits the comparison to DNA associated with the same completed arrival. 
Alternatives that arose under incompatible conditions are not interchangeable merely because they 
occupy the same neuron.
The comparison asks two questions. Does an alternative reach the desired outcome? If it does not, 
does it improve on the current outcome relative to that goal? A useful alternative can be followed and 
examined further. If the examined alternatives do not improve the result, the process can move 
farther back. A nested shortcut may itself be reopened, allowing a change within a subordinate 
process to affect the containing process.
This is the conceptual significance of retaining the long route. The route contains decisions that 
remain useful when the original final answer is no longer sufficient. A shortcut without recoverable 
internals saves time but offers little basis for this kind of adaptation. A shortcut with its supporting 
structure preserves a compressed working process and a path back into the choices from which it 
was made.
Several subtleties follow. A shard's immediate next destination is not necessarily the completed 
outcome of the continuation it begins. Comparing useful alternatives may require access to 
remembered outcomes or to a bounded way of evaluating them. Likewise, equal endpoint identities 
do not establish identical arrivals. The system needs the context and dependencies that make an 
alternative eligible.
The meaning of improvement is also task-dependent. A numerical distance can serve a small 
arithmetic experiment. It cannot automatically decide whether an explanation is more accurate, a 
plan more durable, or a question more useful. The owner's mind-map record includes qualities such 
as completeness, efficiency, durability, and energy. A real adaptation process must use criteria 
appropriate to its present goal.
The proposed method should not be mistaken for a proof that a sequence of locally improving steps 
always reaches a satisfactory solution. Some problems may require exploring an uncertain or 
temporarily worse route. Some may require changing the original frame. The owner's broader 
accounts include both missing-variable repair and moving into a different tree of interpretation. 
Exhausting the currently known alternatives shows a boundary of available knowledge, not the 
impossibility of any better approach.
An important later extension is to test the new process against other remembered cases. A change 
invented for the present problem may resolve a previous unknown or improve an earlier result. The 
system should ask where else the new relationship applies. This connects recursive adaptation to 
back-population: a local discovery can alter the possibilities available elsewhere without requiring a 
blind recomputation of everything.
The archive reports bounded early step-back demonstrations, but these should be read at their actual 
scope. They do not establish the full recursive method for arbitrary processes. The thesis lies in the 
larger continuity: old experience can supply the internal working material from which a new process 
is constructed. [S09, S11, S14, S23]

14. Recall, relevance, and the feeling pattern
Neuron's account of recall begins with a rejection of indiscriminate search. The owner does not 
imagine a brain considering every memory or every formula whenever it faces a problem. The 
present situation should provide structure that directs attention toward a much smaller relevant 
region. Remembering is an act of access organized by what is being recognized.
The first question is often whether the system has encountered a similar pattern. The pattern may 
concern a problem, a sequence of actions, an outcome, a relationship, or an experience. It need not be 
a match of literal words. Two situations in different domains can share a useful process, while two 
sentences with similar wording can require different processes.
The owner describes rejecting a candidate memory as soon as a decisive mismatch appears. This is a 
useful principle for inspecting candidates, but it is not a complete account of finding them. A system 
that rejects each memory quickly can still visit every memory. The approved scope records preserve 
this distinction: the pattern must help route the system to plausible candidates, and then the 
candidates can be examined efficiently.
The proposed access routes are multiple. A memory may be found through time, through a definition, 
through the outcome of a process, through a recurring problem, or through the relationships 
embodied in its receipt. These routes need not compete as exclusive accounts of memory. A process 
can enter through one cue and then inspect other features to determine whether the retrieved 
experience applies.
The owner's feeling-pattern explanation adds a particularly personal route. The account describes a 
composition of bodily and internal features rather than a simple category such as happy or sad. A 
situation has a recognizable overall pattern. That pattern can bring similar experiences into reach 
before a more deliberate examination of their procedures and outcomes.
The numerical feeling factor used in the monologue is pseudocode. It does not establish a scalar 
measurement of emotion, a known biological mechanism, or an implemented Neuron encoding. The 
essential point is the composition: after a candidate experience is reached, the system checks 
whether the pattern was produced in a relevant way. Two experiences that seem similar at first may 
differ in precisely the feature that matters now.
This distinction prevents feeling from becoming a substitute for evidence. The cue can guide access 
without determining the final answer. A recalled experience may provide a promising starting point, 
an irrelevant association, or a warning that the present problem has been framed badly. Its practical 
value depends on the subsequent comparison.
The owner's contrast between personal feeling-based access and another person's more 
chronological recollection also discourages a universal claim. The source is a report of how the 
owner experiences recall. For Neuron it supplies a research hypothesis about composed internal 
state as an index. The machine would need an explicit account of the represented features, their 
construction, their use in retrieval, and their correction through experience.
The larger architectural principle remains consistent with the rest of the thesis. Relevance should be 
learned and represented, not repeatedly supplied by an omniscient outside search routine. The route

acquire, use, and revise that route within its own structure. [S06, S08, S09, S11, S23]
15. Guessing, evidence, and revisable truth
The owner does not prohibit guessing. The objection is to losing the fact that a guess was made. A 
hypothesis can be essential to discovering a new relationship, choosing a trial, or proceeding under 
incomplete information. It becomes dangerous to the integrity of the system when later activity 
treats it as established knowledge merely because it has been stored or repeated.
Neuron is therefore intended to preserve uncertainty through use. A guessed relation should remain 
identifiable when recalled, when combined with other relations, and when it contributes to a new 
answer. The system must be able to distinguish what it knows from what it currently suspects. If a 
conclusion depends on an uncertain premise, that dependency matters even when the conclusion is 
expressed fluently.
The proposed record of a guess includes its origin and grounds. What suggested it? Which 
observations or relationships support it? Under what conditions is it expected to hold? What result 
would count against it? These questions convert a guess from a disposable utterance into an 
investigable object. They also let the system recognize when it is repeating an old hypothesis whose 
failure is already known.
Confirmation changes the standing of a hypothesis without changing its history. A relationship that 
began as a guess may become well supported within a particular scope. Its earlier uncertainty 
remains part of the record. Likewise, a once-useful relation may later require qualification or 
replacement. The system's current working view can change while preserving how that view 
developed.
The owner's account of truth is consequently operational and revisable. The machine can adopt a 
relationship because the available evidence supports using it. Adoption does not establish absolute 
certainty. A successful outcome supports a process under the observed conditions; broader use 
requires attention to the differences between those conditions and the present case.
The archive also warns against an overly rigid notion of wrongness. An answer can fail to match a 
predetermined expected string while revealing a valid alternative relation or a useful partial 
understanding. This does not make every unexpected output correct. It means the experiment must 
examine what the output actually demonstrates. A failure to match the teacher's preferred answer 
can be either a genuine error or evidence that the evaluation omitted a meaningful possibility.
There is a complementary danger in excessive flexibility. If every outcome can be redescribed as 
progress, the experiment becomes incapable of failing. Neuron's method needs criteria that permit 
both discovery of unexpected structure and rejection of unsupported claims. The process that 
produced an answer, its applicability, and its consequences help make that distinction.
Numerical confidence introduces another layer. A number attached to a guess can summarize 
support, frequency, a policy, or a learned prediction. These are different meanings. The later audit 
finds supplied scoring policies and support ratios in particular components; it does not establish a

calibrated probability of correctness for the whole system. A confidence value must be interpreted 
through how it was produced.
The system must also avoid manufacturing evidence from its own repetitions. Hearing the same 
stored claim again is not an independent observation. Agreement with an existing belief does not by 
itself establish that belief's truth. A prediction needs to be compared with an outcome acquired 
through an appropriate channel, and the relationship between the evidence and the claim must 
remain visible.
The thesis's epistemic ambition is that uncertainty becomes part of the executable structure. It 
should affect whether a process is reused, whether an answer is qualified, whether another question 
is asked, and whether an adopted relation is reconsidered. A guess label printed once is an initial 
interface feature. Persistent uncertainty that changes later decisions is the deeper capability. [S06,

16. Context, answer locations, and practical judgment
An answer location need not contain one final sentence. In the owner's model it can contain or lead to 
a process for finding an answer, together with memories of different answers that were appropriate 
in different circumstances. The identity of a question may remain recognizable while the useful 
response changes with time, state, and the person asking.
The shopping example makes this concrete. A request for a nearby store may be about speed, cost, 
quality, an immediate need, or a special occasion. A useful response depends on what the person 
intends, where they are, what is currently available, and which preferences or constraints are 
actually known. The owner's named people, places, and preferences are illustrations of that 
dependency, not facts to be inserted into the system as personal knowledge.
The sentence therefore identifies a problem whose solution must be situated. The machine may 
already know a relevant answer. It may know a method for obtaining current information. It may 
lack a crucial constraint and need to ask. It may possess an old answer whose freshness is uncertain. 
Each case calls for a different continuation.
This is where definitions, memory, timeline, and reasoning converge. Definitions help interpret what 
the request concerns. Memory supplies relevant prior interactions and procedures. Time helps judge 
whether remembered information remains useful. Reasoning selects what to do with these 
resources. The answer is a product of their coordinated use.
The owner's running example adds another dimension. A system that remembers previous 
conversations about an activity might notice relationships between enjoyment and circumstances. It 
might also remember that a potentially important concern was already raised and answered. A useful 
conversational continuation depends both on the present input and on the history of what has 
already been discussed.
The example illustrates adaptive selection rather than a script. The machine should not ask about the 
same concern indefinitely merely because it once appeared in a memory. It should retain whether the 
question was asked, what the answer was, and whether a later event changes its relevance. The

ability to stop asking a resolved question is part of the same continuity that makes a good follow-up 
possible.
Context-sensitive behavior also requires restraint. A correlation in memory can suggest a question 
without establishing a fact about the present event. The machine might suspect that a circumstance 
contributed to an outcome, but it must distinguish that suspicion from something observed. 
Otherwise personalization becomes invention.
External interfaces fit naturally into this account. A map service, a calendar, a search engine, or a 
language model can provide information or perform a bounded task. The cognitive question is why 
the system selects that source, what it asks for, how it evaluates the result, and how the result 
changes the ongoing process. Merely connecting an external tool does not supply these capabilities.
The later owner prose explicitly allows a future arrangement in which Neuron chooses to consult a 
language model. This refines the earlier independence requirement. The issue is whether the 
external model secretly supplies every missing decision, or whether Neuron has acquired the process 
that recognizes a need, selects a tool, frames a request, and uses the result. An independent system 
can still use instruments.
Practical judgment is the desired outcome of this coordination. The response should fit the real 
problem and its conditions, not merely resemble an answer associated with similar wording. This is 
why the owner treats becoming better than a fancy chatbot as a developmental milestone rather than 
the final purpose of Neuron. [S04, S08, S11, S20]
17. The sixth sense and the possibility of epiphany
The sixth sense is the owner's name for the brain's own input. A system that acts only when an 
external prompt arrives remains dependent on an outside source to initiate every cognitive episode. 
The proposed next stage is for unresolved needs, discrepancies, remembered commitments, or new 
consequences of learning to generate activity from within the system.
This does not require inventing a mysterious source of intention. The records suggest concrete 
origins. An earlier question may remain unresolved. A newly learned relationship may conflict with 
an adopted belief. A result may fail to match what was expected. A time-related condition may 
become relevant. Each can create a reason for another process to begin, provided the system has 
represented the condition and learned or acquired a way to respond.
The owner's epiphany proposal is especially revealing. While working on one problem, the system 
may produce a formula or outcome that does not solve that problem but does fit another unresolved 
need. If the other need remains available in a suitable form, the result can be recognized as useful 
elsewhere. The apparent surprise is a connection between new work and an older open structure.
The machine would need more than a list of forgotten questions. Each unresolved task must retain 
enough information about its desired outcome and constraints to make later recognition possible. A 
new result must have a way to reach relevant unresolved tasks without requiring a perpetual scan of 
every problem ever encountered. The same locality requirement that governs recall and back-
population applies here.

Neuron thesis  ·  24
This proposal connects unexpected discovery to ordinary mechanisms. A formula developed in one 
context becomes available for comparison in another. A pattern supplies an access route. An outcome 
comparator determines whether the result is useful. A new occurrence records the connection. The 
significance of the result changes because the machine recognizes another problem to which it 
applies.
Self-generated input also raises questions of selection. Several unresolved needs can compete for 
attention. Some may be important but presently unanswerable; others may be easy but irrelevant. 
Repeating the same failed investigation indefinitely is not autonomous learning. The system needs a 
way to assess whether new information or a new process justifies another attempt.
Stopping is equally important. A process can finish, become blocked, exhaust its current useful 
alternatives, or yield to something more urgent. An autonomous loop that lacks these distinctions can 
consume resources without improving its knowledge. The owner's emphasis on progress, preserved 
failures, and changed conditions supplies the conceptual basis for a more selective form of 
persistence.
The archive contains work described as sixth-sense mechanisms and later audits of their 
connections. A switch being enabled is not sufficient evidence that meaningful autonomous work 
occurs. The relevant proof must trace an internally detected condition through selection, action, 
evidence gathering, and an effect on later behavior. Otherwise the system may contain dormant 
components or loops whose activity has little cognitive consequence.
The sixth sense therefore names a developmental transition rather than one feature flag. The system 
begins to organize its own unfinished business. Epiphany becomes one possible event within that 
organization: a new piece of structure unexpectedly satisfies an old need because the need was 
preserved in a form the machine could still use. [S08, S11, S12, S21, S23]
18. Sleep, consolidation, and local prediction
The later sleep proposal adds a distinct idea to the architecture. The owner asks whether a period of 
reduced outward activity could be used to update the parts of the system touched by recent 
experience. The analogy is to sleep and dreams, but the computational proposal is selective 
maintenance and learning over changed or recently used structure.
The owner's distinction between back-population and back-propagation matters. Back-population 
concerns applying newly available relationships to relevant existing structure. The later proposal 
uses back-propagation to suggest updating predictive weights in local models. These operations may 
interact, but they are not the same process merely because both revisit prior material.
The imagined predictive models are small and local. A tree, route, or related region could contain a 
model useful when established knowledge ends and a guess is required. The machine would activate 
that model for a targeted uncertainty rather than relying on one undifferentiated predictor for every 
task. Recent outcomes and changes in the region would inform its later adjustment.
The sleep period would focus on what was touched, changed, or invalidated. If a familiar process 
continued to work under unchanged conditions, there might be little reason to revise it. A changed

Neuron thesis  ·  25
relationship, a relocated concept, or a refuted expectation would create a stronger reason. This 
preserves the project's broader commitment to work directed by relevant structure.
The proposal is explicitly uncertain in the owner's source. The owner asks whether the idea would 
work and acknowledges not knowing enough about the internal organization of predictive models to 
settle the question. Claims in the monologue about human sleep, transformer activity, or energy use 
should be read as motivating analogies and conjectures. They are not experimental findings 
established by the Neuron record.
Attention, probability, and learning also need to remain distinct. A way to emphasize relevant inputs 
is not by itself a rule for improving a predictor from outcomes. A set of counts is not automatically a 
transformer. A numerical score is not automatically calibrated confidence. The archive's later audit 
makes this separation explicit because local predictive machinery can otherwise appear more 
developed than its actual update process.
The strongest part of the proposal is its integration with known uncertainty. A predictive 
continuation should announce the boundary between established knowledge and the guessed 
extension. The system should retain which local model produced the guess, what evidence it used, 
and how later outcomes affected it. The owner insists that the machine, other machines, and human 
readers all remain able to identify the output as a guess.
Dream-like internal trials would require a related distinction. A simulated outcome can help explore 
a hypothesis, but it is produced by the system's current model. It cannot be silently counted as 
independent evidence about the external world. Simulation may reveal inconsistency or a promising 
candidate; validation still depends on the kind of claim being made.
Selective consolidation also connects to memory economy. Repeated experience may support a 
compact reusable pattern while individual occurrences remain available at an appropriate level of 
detail. The exact balance between retention, summarization, and accessible history is unresolved. 
The concept of sleep supplies a possible time and mode for that work; it does not remove the need 
for a defensible retention policy.
Within the thesis, sleep represents a separation of immediate use from reflective maintenance. The 
machine can act using its present structure, then revisit recent experience to improve that structure. 
The proposed benefit is cumulative adaptation with bounded local work. Demonstrating that benefit 
requires outcome-based improvement, preserved uncertainty, and measured resource costs rather 
than resemblance to the vocabulary of biological sleep. [S18, S19, S21, S25]
19. Development and independence from the mother
The mother-to-student metaphor describes a dependency that is meant to change over time. Early in 
development, a teacher supplies experiences, lessons, corrections, and support. The student initially 
lacks many of the processes needed to learn effectively. The project's central concern is that this 
temporary arrangement can become permanent if every new difficulty is solved by adding 
intelligence to the surrounding machinery.

The early registration contract describes a deterministic mathematical teacher. It holds an ordered 
curriculum, receives the structure's returned state, and supplies the next small lesson. That contract 
is an important historical foundation, but it is not the entire later design. Subsequent records include 
language teaching, human instruction, assistant-authored graph procedures, and experiments aimed 
at more independent selection and learning.
Across these changes, the stable requirement is transfer of capability. A lesson can legitimately 
supply a process, but the claim must say that the process was supplied. A teacher can correct an 
error, but the student's later behavior must change in a way the student can retain and use. A worker 
can design a learning procedure, but the machine has not independently invented that procedure 
merely because it executes it.
The owner describes an early ask-then-show teaching pattern. The learner is first given an 
opportunity to expose what it can do at the edge of its knowledge. The teacher then provides a name, 
definition, example, or correction. Further questions can test whether relationships beyond the exact 
taught line are available. This method is intended to keep the teaching intervention distinguishable 
from what the learner subsequently derives.
Definitions are particularly important during this stage. A word introduced without usable meaning 
can improve surface vocabulary while leaving interpretation shallow. A definition provides relations 
and distinctions that later processes can use. Yet even a rich teacher-supplied definition is supplied 
knowledge. Its existence does not prove that the student can infer comparable definitions on its own.
The developmental criterion becomes more demanding as the system grows. Can it select a relevant 
process without the teacher naming it? Can it form a question that exposes a useful missing variable? 
Can it apply a newly acquired relation to a case withheld during teaching? Can it discover that a 
previously successful procedure no longer applies? Each is a concrete way to examine whether 
responsibility has moved inward.
Disconnection is a powerful test of that transfer. If the claimed capability stops working when the 
corresponding external helper is disabled, the capability may still belong to the helper. The test must 
be specific: removing all sensory input or all physical execution would be a different intervention. 
What matters is whether the external source of the claimed cognitive decision remains necessary.
The later possibility of consulting a language model is consistent with this distinction. A mature 
student can ask an external source for information while owning the decision to ask and the process 
for evaluating the answer. The teacher's disappearance as an indispensable internal decision-maker 
does not require the disappearance of every external tool.
Development also requires continuity of the living structure. The owner repeatedly asks for additive 
work, retained experience, and experiments on copies before changes are integrated. A succession of 
clean demonstrations in freshly reset systems cannot substitute for showing that a persistent learner 
continues to work as it acquires and revises knowledge. Accumulated state is part of the object being 
studied.
The ultimate aspiration is that the system acquires enough learning capability to participate in its 
own further development. This is stronger than executing stored procedures and stronger than 
selecting among them. It asks whether experience can improve the processes that turn further

20. A computer built in its own language
The owner frequently describes Neuron as its own computer. This characterization follows from the 
breadth of the graph-as-code claim. If the structure can express reading, comparison, selection, 
writing, composition, and continuation, then tools can be built from those operations. More complex 
processes can be built from the tools, and procedures for constructing processes can themselves 
become part of the structure.
The examples of grep, cat, and sed are best understood as descriptions of work. The request is not to 
store the name of an existing utility at a node and then invoke an external implementation. It is to 
determine what the operation does and realize that behavior through neuronal runs. Finding a 
match, returning stored content, or replacing a selected structure are different operations that 
require actual executable organization.
The same principle applies when a needed capability appears to be missing from the primitive 
vocabulary. The owner's standard fix is to ask what the proposed external helper would actually do, 
identify the simpler operations it needs, and build the missing behavior in Neuron code where 
possible. The obstacle becomes an opportunity to expand the system's own code base.
This method requires careful separation between irreducible substrate operations and higher-level 
procedures. Merely declaring a complicated behavior to be primitive can hide the same external 
intelligence under a new name. Conversely, refusing every generic execution primitive would make it 
impossible to describe a physical implementation at all. The substantive question is whether the 
primitive boundary is explicit and whether the claimed reasoning, selection, and learning reside 
above it in the graph.
Compilation is a natural long-term consequence of the proposal. A process that constructs another 
executable process can make the graph increasingly capable of extending itself. The output of one run 
may become the structure through which a later run proceeds. A correction can alter a procedure 
rather than merely annotate it. This is the point at which the distinction between stored knowledge 
and program construction becomes especially thin.
The thesis does not assume that a graph capable of some arithmetic has thereby demonstrated a 
general compiler or universal computation. Such claims require particular expressive mechanisms 
and evidence. The archive includes supplied arithmetic and NAND-based programs, but their 
existence establishes only the capabilities actually demonstrated. The broader computer remains an 
architectural destination.
The mathematical repertoire contemplated by the project is correspondingly broad. An owner 
request to expand a narrow search space led to an assistant catalog of 73 candidate families. It 
ranged across algebraic transformations, symbolic composition, constraints, uncertainty, causal 
reasoning, language structure, memory, inquiry, and program verification. The catalog matters as 
evidence that the project was seeking reusable mathematical ingredients rather than one universal

Several jobs must remain distinct within that repertoire. A mathematical form used to locate a 
concept has a different role from a procedure used to search for an applicable relation, and both 
differ from a method used to assess evidence for the relation. All might be represented in the same 
medium without becoming interchangeable. The unified architecture needs these distinctions 
precisely because it asks the same medium to perform many kinds of work.
The project's evolving vocabulary also matters here. Words such as read, copy, infer, learn, and 
understand can conceal several distinct operations. A worker may believe it has implemented 
learning when it has implemented storage, or believe it has implemented understanding when it has 
selected a canned response. The later cognitive vocabulary attempts to assign explicit meanings, 
operands, and evidence requirements to such operations.
That glossary is assistant-authored and is not itself executable Neuron code. Its value is 
methodological: it gives people a more precise way to describe what a process must accomplish. It 
helps prevent a broad label from standing in for missing behavior. The machine still needs the 
corresponding executable structure.
A mature Neuron language would therefore need two kinds of clarity. Internally, its structures must 
have definite operational consequences. Externally, its explanations must distinguish what was 
received, stored, inferred, predicted, tested, and changed. The first makes the machine run. The 
second makes its development intelligible enough to evaluate.
The proposed computer is unusual in its ambition to unify these layers through experience. 
Programs are not only artifacts written by a developer; they are intended to become retained 
products of learning. Their use leaves receipts, their failures supply evidence, and their internal 
decisions remain available for adaptation. The machine's code base would be a growing history of 
things it has learned how to do. [S02, S05, S07, S08, S23, S25]
21. Memory economy and the physical cost of the 
thesis
The promise of inexpensive familiar execution depends on how experience is represented physically. 
A mathematical identity does not guarantee a compact encoding, and a short conceptual route does 
not guarantee cheap storage access. The system must preserve the structure required by its theory 
without expanding every reused process into a fresh copy of all its internals.
The archive contains a concrete storage diagnosis that illustrates the issue. An October 3 report 
attributed most of the measured database allocation to gate expressions and their unique index. 
Receipt representations embedded child expression text recursively. The report identified a 
particularly large receipt whose expanded expression occupied more than a megabyte even though 
its immediate child references were much smaller.

Neuron thesis  ·  29
The significance is structural amplification. If several parent receipts each embed the same large 
child expression, sharing exists conceptually but is defeated by serialization. Repetition then 
increases storage far beyond the amount of genuinely new experience. The proposed repair was to 
retain shared child references while preserving exact identity and reconstructability.
The small size of immediate references does not mean that the whole memory can be represented in 
those few bytes. The referenced children still require storage, as do the rules and data needed to 
interpret them. The valid saving comes from storing shared structure once and referring to it, not 
from pretending the supporting structure has disappeared.
Exact identity must also survive compact representation. A short fingerprint can help locate 
candidates, but an accidental collision must not merge distinct receipts. The thesis requires 
recoverable distinctions between processes, arrivals, and outcomes. Compression that destroys those 
distinctions may improve a size metric while weakening the cognitive model.
A second issue is the distinction between representation and retention. Sharing repeated structure 
solves one kind of waste. Deciding how long to retain an occurrence solves another. The owner asks 
about working memory, recent memory, and compressed accessible long-term memory. These 
categories suggest different access and detail requirements, but the archive presents their mature 
policy as unfinished.
Common symbols expose the difficulty sharply. If every use of a period produces a full independent 
memory directly under the period neuron, that location can become an enormous historical 
bottleneck. The owner explicitly raises this problem. The requirement that punctuation have a 
representation does not imply that every occurrence must duplicate every associated sentence in one 
local collection.
Back-population and shortcut invalidation add update costs. A newly corrected relationship may 
affect many dependent processes. Selective propagation must find the affected region and preserve 
enough dependency information to avoid stale reuse. This costs storage and work, but omitting it 
would make the shortcut unreliable when the knowledge on which it depends changes.
The same accounting applies to local predictive models and internally generated work. Many small 
models can still become a large total system. A collection of unresolved tasks can still consume 
unbounded resources if nothing retires, consolidates, or becomes dormant. Locality is a design 
principle whose aggregate consequences must be measured.
The strongest performance claim available at the conceptual level is therefore conditional. Neuron 
may make frequently reused processes inexpensive by preparing structure during learning and 
preserving efficient access routes. The benefit depends on reuse, representation, locality, validity 
maintenance, and the cost of updates. The owner's numerical visions of enormous neuronal 
populations and very low energy remain aspirations rather than measured properties.
This does not reduce memory economy to an implementation detail. It is part of the thesis itself. 
Experience becomes a useful computational resource only if the machine can retain, reach, and revise 
it at a cost compatible with continued development. A system that grows incapable of using its own 
history has lost the continuity it was designed to obtain. [S03, S07, S11, S18, S19, S21]

23. The research method implied by the system
Neuron's theory implies a demanding experimental method because the same output can be 
produced by very different mechanisms. A student may recall a taught answer, execute a supplied 
rule, compose known relations, or discover a new relation. An external teacher may supply the 
decisive step while the graph records the result. Evaluation must identify which of these actually 
occurred.
The first requirement is an inventory of what was supplied before the test. Relevant vocabulary, 
decoder mappings, definitions, procedures, examples, and prior observations all matter. Without that 
inventory, a new-looking answer can be mistaken for new knowledge. The little incident 
demonstrates why the source of the answer must be traced across every route by which it could have 
entered.
The second requirement is to define the desired behavior before implementing the demonstration. A 
test that merely checks whether code follows its own design can establish consistency with that 
design while saying little about the owner's intended outcome. The experiment needs a concrete 
problem, observable consequences, and conditions under which the candidate explanation would 
fail.
The third requirement is to use the real boundary through which the capability is supposed to 
operate. If ordinary input cannot reach a learner, an internal API demonstration does not establish 
ordinary learning. If a question is generated but its answer does not return to the process that 
needed it, the inquiry loop is incomplete. End-to-end evidence matters because the thesis concerns 
coordinated development, not a museum of individually functioning components.
The owner's copy-first method protects the accumulated structure while making changes reviewable. 
A fresh copy preserves the starting state. The intervention can be recorded, the resulting behavior 
observed, and the tested change integrated without replacing intervening work in the living system. 
This is an experimental discipline as well as a practical response to the value of persistent 
experience.
Useful comparisons isolate what learning contributes. A system with adaptation enabled can be 
compared with an otherwise identical system whose learned state remains fixed, and with one that 
only recalls prior cases. New combinations, changed conditions, and later contradictions can expose 
whether the system has acquired a transferable process. These comparisons are proposals from the 
audit, not experiments performed for this manuscript.
Mechanistic evidence must accompany performance. A successful response is more convincing when 
the receipt shows which graph structures selected and performed the work, what external help 
remained, and what changed afterward. A system that changes an internal parameter should show 
how the change follows from relevant evidence and how it affects a later case. Mutability alone is not 
learning.

Resource measures are also part of the test. Preparation time, execution work, memory growth, 
update cost, and the extent of structure visited should be measured separately. An apparently fast 
answer can depend on an expensive earlier sweep; a compact immediate output can leave a large 
persistent expansion. The owner's claims about shortcuts and locality require accounting across the 
whole process.
Generalization should be described at the level actually tested. A relation inferred across known 
words is different from a nameless concept inferred before labeling. A procedure that survives a 
restart is different from one that survives removal of its external decision-maker. A successful local 
repair is different from general self-revision. Precise categories allow progress to accumulate 
without turning every result into a claim about complete cognition.
Finally, the research method must remain corrigible. An unexpected answer may expose a faulty test, 
an overlooked sense, or a useful new relation. A passing result may expose hidden teaching when 
examined more carefully. The point of the record is to preserve enough evidence that these 
interpretations can change. Neuron asks the machine to learn from its history; the research program 
must do the same. [S10, S11, S13, S15, S21, S25]
24. Conclusion: the proposed unity of knowledge and 
action
The deepest claim in Neuron is that knowledge can become the organization of action. An experience 
is not only stored as content that a separate intelligence must later interpret. It leaves relations, 
routes, conditions, and outcomes that can participate in future computation. A learned process 
becomes something the system can enter and use. A failed process becomes something it can 
examine. A corrected relation becomes a change in what later runs can do.
This gives the project a coherent account of familiarity. A familiar problem is one for which the 
machine has already acquired relevant structure. The work of learning has left behind a usable route, 
a known relation, or a parameterized process. Familiar execution can be cheap because the system 
does not need to rediscover everything that made the process possible.
It also gives the project an account of novelty. A new problem need not be wholly unlike every earlier 
problem. It may differ at one decision, one missing relation, one condition, or one assumption. The 
retained derivation makes those differences accessible. Recursive adaptation proposes that the 
machine can reopen the relevant part while preserving the work that still applies.
Memory, in this view, is the bridge between the two. It preserves the experience from which familiar 
processes were formed and the internal structure from which unfamiliar processes may be 
constructed. Timeline and state make that bridge historically meaningful. They distinguish what was 
known from what was learned later, what was predicted from what was observed, and what 
happened once from what can be expected again.
Language connects the system to people and to recorded knowledge, but its role is broader than 
producing text. An utterance points toward work. A definition supplies constraints. A question seeks

Neuron thesis  ·  34
a missing dependency. A response can express the outcome of an investigation or begin another one. 
The machine's understanding is to be judged by the processes these forms reliably engage.
Uncertainty is equally structural. A guess must remain a guess wherever it is used until evidence 
changes its standing. A known relation must retain its scope. A disagreement must remain available 
for investigation. This makes trustworthiness part of the learning mechanism rather than a 
decorative qualification added to the final sentence.
The sixth sense extends the architecture from responding to organizing its own unfinished work. A 
preserved unknown can become a reason to act when new evidence or a new process appears. 
Epiphany is then a possible relation between current activity and an earlier need. Sleep and local 
prediction add a proposed mode for consolidating recent experience and preparing explicitly 
uncertain continuations.
The mother-to-student transition unifies the developmental ambition. At first, the environment 
supplies much of the work. Over time, the student's structure should take responsibility for selection, 
execution, inquiry, evaluation, and revision. The decisive question is whether this transfer actually 
happens. Adding capability to the surrounding teacher can make the demonstration more impressive 
while leaving the student unchanged.
The archive's failures illuminate this criterion. Supplied answers mistaken for discoveries, outside 
helpers mistaken for graph-owned cognition, and isolated components mistaken for a closed learning 
cycle all obscure the location of capability. The owner's repeated corrections insist that the machine's 
own structure must carry the explanatory burden.
The research record does not yet establish the strongest vision. It does not prove that Neuron 
reproduces the human brain, possesses subjective experience, or will achieve the anticipated scale 
and efficiency. Those ambitions cannot be settled by the simplicity of the verbal model or by the 
owner's confidence in it. They require mechanisms and observations that remain open.
What the record does preserve is a substantive architectural thesis. A cognitive system is proposed 
as a persistent, developing organization in which relationships locate knowledge, runs perform work, 
receipts preserve experience, and later processes transform that experience into further capability. 
The distinctiveness lies in the intended integration: memory is recoverable computation; code is 
reusable experience; inquiry is a process the system can acquire; and learning changes the structures 
through which learning itself proceeds.
The central experiment can therefore be stated without reducing the project to a chatbot or inflating 
it into a completed brain. An experience enters the system. The system interprets it through its 
existing structure, identifies or investigates what is missing, and changes that structure on the basis 
of an outcome. The change persists. When a relevant new situation arrives, the system acts 
differently for reasons recoverable in its own history. Eventually, it should also recognize when to 
begin that cycle itself.
If that cycle becomes reliable, transferable, and increasingly independent, the thesis gains substance. 
Its significance would be the conversion of experience into an enduring capacity to act, question, and 
revise. That is the Neuron system the owner's prose describes, and that is the standard by which its 
realization should be understood. [S02, S04, S06, S08, S09, S11, S19, S20, S21, S23]

Appendix A. A reference lexicon
The definitions below summarize usage in this manuscript. They are editorial explanations of the 
source material, not new primitive instructions, a ratified storage schema, or a replacement for the 
project's versioned vocabulary.
Neuron and gate
A neuron is a realized location in the mathematical structure. Gate emphasizes its identity as a place a 
run can enter. A storage identifier may refer to the neuron without being its mathematical identity. 
What happens there depends on its executable relationships and the arriving process.
Expression and formula
An expression can identify a location or represent a composed result. Formula has broader use in the 
owner's prose: it can describe a relationship, a process, a test, a selection method, or a composition of 
other formulas. The intended work should be specified whenever the broad word risks concealing an 
unresolved operation.
Relationship and placement
A relationship constrains how locations are connected or transformed under stated conditions. 
Placement is the assignment or discovery of a location consistent with relevant relationships. 
Placement can be provisional and later revised. A convenient algebraic fit does not by itself validate a 
semantic relationship.
DNA and shard
DNA denotes local executable relationships and continuation structure. A shard is an individually 
selectable part of that structure. The metaphor communicates locally retained operational 
information; it does not assert a molecular implementation.
Arrival and remainder
Arrival is the relevant state and path context by which a run reaches a location. Remainder is the 
still-pending portion of the work. Their exact encodings are implementation questions. Their 
conceptual purpose is to prevent distinct uses of the same location from being treated as identical.
Run and receipt
A run is an execution through neuronal structure. A receipt preserves the realized process in a form 
that supports identity, recall, and further use. A receipt of a shortcut should record the shortcut 
actually used while retaining access to its supporting derivation.

Memory and occurrence
Memory is retained experience or content accessible through the system's structure. An occurrence 
is a particular happening in time. Several occurrences can share a process or receipt identity while 
differing in source, conditions, expectations, or outcomes.
Timeline and state
Timeline organizes occurrences and their temporal relations. State is the relevant configuration of 
knowledge, assumptions, ongoing work, and circumstances at a time. A timestamp alone does not 
capture all the state required to interpret an event.
Definition and Relationships section
A definition is usable descriptive structure associated with a location, potentially including senses, 
examples, grammatical roles, and other distinctions. The Relationships section is the owner's 
requirement that definitions gain an updatable account of relations arriving at the location. 
Provenance and revision fields discussed in the archive include assistant proposals and bounded 
implementations.
Nameless concept
A nameless concept is an unnamed but relationally constrained location. Its partial meaning can be 
investigated before a surface word is taught. The ability to output an already supplied word does not 
by itself demonstrate this capability.
Back-population
Back-population applies newly available relationships or corrections to relevant preexisting 
structure. Its scope is determined by applicability and dependencies. It is distinct from an 
indiscriminate full-corpus sweep and from the later proposal for updating predictive weights.
Shortcut and long route
A shortcut reuses established work without repeating all of its internal derivation. The long route 
preserves the working structure and support that make correction and adaptation possible. Both are 
part of the intended architecture.
Step-back and recursive adaptation
Step-back follows a retained process toward earlier choices and examines eligible local alternatives. 
Recursive adaptation can reopen a nested process, alter a useful decision, and return the new result 
to the containing process. The owner's examples do not establish that local improvement alone 
guarantees a solution.

Pattern and feeling pattern
A pattern is a relevant organization of relations, features, or processes that can guide access and 
comparison. A feeling pattern is the owner's proposed composite of bodily and internal features used 
as an initial memory cue. Its numerical examples are illustrative; no fixed encoding is assumed here.
Question and answer location
A question seeks information required by an ongoing or proposed process. An answer location may 
contain an available answer, several context-dependent possibilities, or a process for obtaining what 
is needed. The question's wording and the operation it serves must remain distinguishable.
Guess and adopted belief
A guess is a proposed continuation, relation, explanation, or result whose uncertainty is retained. An 
adopted belief is something the system currently accepts for use within a scope. Adoption does not 
erase provenance or establish absolute truth.
Progress and outcome
An outcome is a result or effect of a process. Progress is improvement relative to a goal and its 
constraints. Speed, confidence, reduced uncertainty, and familiar-looking output are not 
automatically equivalent to progress.
Sixth sense and epiphany
The sixth sense is internally generated input or task initiation. Epiphany is the proposed recognition 
that a new result or process satisfies another preserved unresolved need. Both require mechanisms 
for relevance, selection, and stopping.
Sleep and local predictor
Sleep names a proposed mode of selective consolidation and revision over recently used or changed 
structure. A local predictor is a bounded model associated with a relevant region and used to 
produce an explicitly identified guess. These are proposals in the recovered owner text, not evidence 
of a completed sleeping or dreaming organism.
Mother, teacher, and student
Mother names the developmental support outside the learner. Teacher names the source of lessons 
or feedback. Student names the developing neuronal system. The intended transition is increasing 
ownership of cognitive processes by the student, while allowing legitimate sensory input and tool 
use.

Coverage and limits of this edition
This edition preserves the major conceptual strands recovered from the owner prose: mathematical 
identity; the graph as code; local DNA and arrival; developmental birth and propagation; receipts; 
memory, time, and state; definitions and nameless concepts; language and contextual action; 
composed reasoning; questions; recursive shortcuts; pattern and feeling recall; durable uncertainty; 
the sixth sense and epiphany; sleep and local prediction; maternal independence; and memory 
economy.
It synthesizes these strands rather than reproducing the owner's monologues or writing in the 
owner's voice. Explanatory connections made by the manuscript are interpretations of the cited 
material. They do not create new owner requirements or settle missing formulas.
The manuscript does not claim exhaustive retrieval of Cortex, completion of every referenced 
historical experiment, or fresh validation of archived runtime reports. The recovered source pages 
include duplicate passages, assistant proposals, and descriptions of files not available at their former 
local paths. Those limits are why source type, date, and implementation status remain explicit.
The finished document is a readable reference to the system's thesis and its research boundary. The 
source copies make it possible to challenge the synthesis against the material from which it was 
written. That recoverability is part of the same principle the project asks of Neuron: retain the path 
by which a conclusion was reached so it can be examined and improved.
