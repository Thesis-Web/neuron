**Yes—the core computational idea is feasible.** A graph can hold executable behavior, and a sufficiently expressive graph system can construct and transform its own programs. Your proposed developmental transition is credible. Whether this particular system learns useful cognition, and how much faster it becomes, remain separate experimental questions.

I also think your criticism identifies a real failure in our approach: when an LLM implements the missing behavior in Python, it can produce the requested output while leaving the architectural hypothesis untested.

**“The graph is the code” can have a precise meaning.** The graph holds the instructions, dependencies, choices, bindings, and continuations that determine what happens. A small execution mechanism follows those structures. Graph-based computation already has rigorous foundations: Lafont’s interaction combinators demonstrate universal computation using a very small set of graph interaction rules. That establishes feasibility for this general class of machine; it does not establish that Neuron’s current primitives are sufficient. Some execution substrate remains: something physically reads memory, follows links, allocates storage, and performs primitive transitions. Even a compiler written inside the graph ultimately runs on that substrate. **The meaningful boundary is where the decisions and operation sequences reside.** Python can implement a generic primitive without deciding which definitions qualify as verbs or how a memory belongs on the timeline.

Your proposed speed advantage also has a sound basis. Computing what is already knowable ahead of use is an established technique called **partial evaluation**: specialize a general procedure using known information, leaving less work for later. It can even derive compiled programs from interpreters. [Partial Evaluation, Whole-Program Compilation (https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10559)](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10559>)

For Neuron, I would distinguish three situations:

| Situation | What learning can prepare | What use still requires |
|---|---|---|
| A known relationship | Destination and applicable route | Follow the stored relationship |
| A known procedure with new inputs | Operation order, dependencies, reusable subruns | Bind inputs and execute the remaining steps |
| A genuinely new problem | Previously learned components | Find or construct a composition, test it, retain the result |

That middle case matters. Knowing the multiplication procedure cannot precompute every possible multiplication result. But it can eliminate rediscovering the procedure. Likewise, substituting a gate into an already materialized relationship can be a lookup; substituting into an unfamiliar expression may require new computation.

**Hopping is itself computation.** Its advantage comes when a short traversal replaces a much larger search or repeated derivation. Merely expressing an expensive algorithm as many tiny hops does not make the underlying work disappear.

The same applies to back-population. Your spreadsheet analogy is useful: update affected dependencies when knowledge changes, then read the prepared result cheaply. Incremental computation provides established methods for reusing previous work across changes, including iterative computations. [Differential Dataflow (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/differential-dataflow/)](<https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/differential-dataflow/>)

However, precomputing everything would create enormous storage and update costs. The promising hypothesis is **selective preparation and reuse**: realize useful relationships, share repeated structure, and update affected dependencies. At large scale, memory layout and access locality matter enormously; a chain of scattered database lookups can be slower than a compact compiled calculation. Energy savings therefore need measurement too.
