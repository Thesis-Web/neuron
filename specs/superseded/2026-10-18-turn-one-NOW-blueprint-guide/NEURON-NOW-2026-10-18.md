Everything here is the owner's current hypothesis, not proven fact. Anything marked **OPEN** isn't decided yet; the builder decides those (§17).*

## 1. What Neuron is

Neuron is a computer that builds itself and talks to itself, and is thought to reason, understand and learn on its own. It is made of **neurons**: database nodes built to do these tasks outside the normal realm of code.

It has three parts:

- **Code.** It performs the actions that the meanings of words (or code) stand for.
- **Math.** Problem solving, which adds to its reasoning.
- **Language.** A graph of the root meanings under every human language. Any human language can be put onto it without the brain changing.

**The key idea.** A human word is shorthand code. A word's **meaning is the action it causes**. A definition only *describes* that meaning. Meanings are hard to find because they are written down nowhere: over human history, shorthand code has been described with more shorthand code, and the root was lost.

It learns slowly. Once it understands something, it runs almost like a deterministic function. On the surface it looks like a chatbot. Underneath, parallel runs turn determinism into cognition while using little energy. That cognition comes from **asking itself questions** about its own state, history and memories, and from **checking sources**.

**Why it exists:** Cortex couldn't take in data fast enough and judge whether it was true or contradictory. That is the brain's job now. Nothing from Cortex carries over.

## 2. Turn one: the baby

Turn one builds **the baby that can become Isaac Newton**. It has every process it needs to grow with nobody ever helping, and none of the data.

On day one it must be able to:

- learn, build, adjust, test, and write new processes that improve itself, which **nobody told it to make**;
- keep a state timeline;
- build new neurons and organize them;
- have memories;
- relate meanings to other meanings;
- check a source, and diff what the source says against its current state;
- sense balance or imbalance in an outcome, to tell whether it made progress or reached its goal.

**Nothing is left out of turn one.** Talking, the sixth, sleep and the HC, sources, the timeline, guesses and beliefs all exist at their bare-minimum root level, and the brain can change each one as it finds better ways. The HC doesn't have to exist on day one, but the brain must be able to build it itself. Anything that needs a later build turn will never work.

**Its goal:** become smarter and more efficient. It is never satisfied with being helped. It wants to do things itself, and asks for help only when it can't.

## 3. The laws

1. **It acts on what it knows, and what it knows grows.** It learns from sources, forms ideas and hypotheses, tests them, and shows its outputs for confirmation.
2. **Nothing it says is canned or told to it.** Words without meaning are broken code.
3. **No walker or script tells it what to do.** It decides, then either measures the result itself or asks us whether it was right.
4. **It is not subservient to a user.** It runs on a sacrificial dev stack: if it runs away, pull the plug and start over.
5. **It has core values designed for good behavior, and they cannot be untaught.** Who lays them: **OPEN**.
6. **The graph is the code.** Used in the right pattern, the graph works the silicon. It may start on SQL with a bottom layer, and it finds better ways as it grows.
7. **The output leaves the graph.** Whether the sixth sits inside the graph and feeds it inputs (so one input becomes many): **OPEN**.
8. **The LLM builder writes only the first code.** After turn one, the brain writes all of its own. The rule is on the builder, not the brain: an LLM writing py, sh or sql drifts into building a chatbot and never gives the brain control of itself.
9. **It aims to stop needing us as fast as possible.** Builders add error and drift at every turn.
10. **It never searches everything at once (`*.*`).** It finds things the cheapest way first: through relationships, pattern-matching formulas, and its state and memories.
11. **Tables are allowed only if they work at scale.** Watch for bloat.
12. **No cheating.** Never do just enough to call something done. Null, `any` and "all" exist in code for a reason, but using them to skirt the work is forbidden.
13. **Everything here is a hypothesis.** It gets dialed in as we make progress.

## 4. Meaning, words and labels

- **Language is actions, or purpose.** The letters of a word mean nothing; what matters is the action the word excites. (If letters mattered, the world would have one language.)
- **Points don't have to be words.** The points in the graph matter most, but they can be expressions, numbers, formulas, equations or logs. Get them right, and the graph shows meaning in math: this is 1/x and that is x, so this is the reciprocal of that.
- **Where words live.** Language words live in the **encoder and decoder**. While the bottom is SQL, the action words (insert, delete, move, get, ...) also live in the graph. As the brain gets smarter, it does those actions on the silicon directly.
- **A point's meaning is the action it does.** That action depends on its description (definition), and on how the DNA triggers for the shard that was touched. One point can do many things, depending on how it is used and which shards are touched in what order. Where the action physically lives (a trigger, the DNA, the relationships, or the rigid layer): **OPEN**.
- **One meaning, one gate.** Two words with exactly the same meaning may share a gate through the encoder. Two *meanings* on one gate means a formula is wrong. Near-same is not the same: "thin" and "fine" are different meanings.
- **Definitions.** Every definition follows one semi-rigid schema, with relationship sections that fill themselves. Definitions come from sources (dictionaries, reference docs, WordNet), and some are derived by the brain from what it believes is true. The schema's sections: **OPEN**.
- **The starting set** is the root primitives, plus the 65 primes (the actions every language has) built from them. Some primes are two or more actions combined. Pseudocode: on = y, off = −y; yes = x, no = 1/x. Words needed early: be, are, to, who, what, when, where, how, is, can, you, me, I, and more.
- **Words, math and definitions are learned together**, not math first. Relationships come from meanings, meanings are described by definitions, and definitions need words.

## 5. The graph

- **The point of the graph is instant reach.** The brain never looks anything up, sorts a table, or sweeps. The opposite of up is simply *there*.
- **Math relationships are the best known way to get that.** The inverse of up lives at the point the inverse formula gives. The simplest expressions give the **location**, and they are a shortcut to the formulas. Don't get hung up on which expression is which. Any other way that is just as instant is acceptable.
- **Points move whenever a relationship's strength changes:** a new relationship, a truth shift, being wrong and then right. There is no schedule and no rule; the graph shifts.
- **Numbers.** There is no number line, and no neuron for every number. The brain holds about a hundred numbers: 1 to 10 plus the skip counts (10s, 20s, 50s, 100s, 1,000s, on up through millions and billions). Because it understands what numbers *mean*, it can reach any number without a place for it.
- **Zero:** **OPEN** (see §18).

## 6. The neuron

A neuron is:

- a **gate**, its address, reached instantly;
- **DNA**, made of many shards;
- **triggers**;
- a **remainder**;
- **memories**, starting with the **definition memory**, which must exist when the neuron is spawned so its relationships have somewhere to land. A gate has a definition (its self-filling relationships) even before it has a label or a source definition. The second memory a neuron starts with was not named: **OPEN**.

**Spawning is automatic.** A remainder with no gate spawns one. If relationships are done right, most newborns already sit at a point known through a relationship; only the very first few may have none.

**Reasoning neurons** are unknown and were never reached. The current thought: many ways out, each asking a different question, all landing on a **scratch pad** that is then compiled.

## 7. Shards and the hop

- **How a run arrives picks the shard**, deterministically.
- **The word after is the remainder**: where the run goes next. The remainder is only what gets you to the next gate.
- **The shard stores the way to the next gate.** That is why the shard exists, and it's how hops took 1–2 µs. If no shard exists for this arrival, one is written.

## 8. Triggers: where reasoning starts

- **Triggers are the questions.** A trigger is the thing in a run that makes the brain ask questions and reason. There is no limit on how many fire, and one may start 50 to 100 runs.
- **Trigger runs branch off without disrupting the main run**, which keeps going.
- **A trigger may simply be extra remainders on a shard:** one or two more ways out that always start other runs. Not tried yet.
- **Some questions decide which question to ask.** The questions themselves get better over time.
- **Example:** "Do you know what happened here?"
  1. What is "here"? Do I know? If not, ask back.
  2. "This piece of paper." Look in memory for papers worked on recently.
  3. Found the skyscraper equation. "Is that what you mean?"
  4. Yes. Recall what was done on it two weeks ago.

## 9. A run

- **Remainders drive the main run; triggers start the other runs.**
- **`1 + 1 =`, walked through:**
  1. There is a gate for **plus** and a gate for **equals**. Their meanings are the code for what the computer actually does to add and to equal.
  2. **One** sits wherever one's point is on the graph (not yet known).
  3. Start at the one gate. The remainder (plus) takes the run to the plus gate.
  4. In the plus gate, the run hits the shard for "one, plus", goes out to one, then into the equals gate. The triggers along the way are the actions that plus and equals do.
  5. **If there is a two gate:** the brain asks "have I done 1 + 1 = before?", finds it in memory, and it's at two. Done.
  6. **If there is no two gate:** equals must know to spawn it, with a write and a memory.
- **The answer is neither the accumulated run nor just the last neuron.** The run splits into many runs, and the answer is **derived** from them by reasoning: understanding, and balance, inequality, progress, probability and statistics equations. Running the question straight through the neurons only gives the question back.
- **Choosing the route:**
  - **Known gate:** go straight to it.
  - **A process that has to go the long way:** it goes the long way.
  - **A known substitution formula:** plug in the variables, go to that gate, and run it there, so the run still gets its parallel reasoning and checks.

## 10. Physical actions and storage

- **Physical actions:** anything a computer can do in code, such as write, read, get, grep, list. Anything.
- **Storage:** whatever does it right. It must be cheap, not bloat, be very fast, and let the brain recurse, step back and forward, and see patterns. Use SQL if SQL can do that; build something new if it can't. SQL has been the choice so far. The owner still thinks the brain should work the silicon itself.

## 11. Memory, receipts and the timeline

- **A memory is held once.** It moves between **active**, **persistent** (always remembered) and **long-term** (condensed, tucked away, retrievable). It keeps its relationships so it can be found fast.
- **A receipt is a small pointer to a memory**, stored wherever it is needed, so the memory is never rewritten.
- **Fresh memories are exact.** The HC condenses them (about daily). Older ones condense down to **highlights** (a feeling, a sense, a smell, an exclamation), which are just enough to **rebuild the run**. One memory sparks the next until the whole memory is back. This is how bloat is fought.
- **The same run again** goes in the same row, with a new timestamp. The brain needs to know "done a lot, and correct", not the exact count.
- **A truth shift** happens when a node moves: the run's **shape in 3D** changes, and that change is the truth shift. It gets a new row, and the memory records what was held true then and what is true now. (Keeping 6–10 shifts is an arbitrary number.)
- **The timeline:** every memory is tied to state and time, in order, so the brain can say "I did this Wednesday, this last Friday, this seven months ago at 2 p.m."

## 12. Truth, guesses and the unknown

- **Grounded truth = reference sources:** textbooks, dictionaries, encyclopedias (to a degree), thesauruses, benchtop references. **Never LLMs. Never the owner.**
- **"Makes sense", or common sense, is reasoning by inequality:** is this closer to the desired outcome, further away, or balanced?
- **Guesses are in from day one**, recorded and announced. With no guess there's no hypothesis, and with no hypothesis there's no learning. Guesses are often wrong, and that's expected.
- **At an unknown, the run ends:** "I got this far, I don't know the rest." It may guess forward along likely nodes. More likely, it finds **completed runs with a similar shape** and reasons by metaphor ("this is how I use a chainsaw; is a hand saw similar?").
- **Its outcomes can be right or wrong.** It accepts corrections and confirmations, and keeps or adjusts its outcomes.

## 13. Learning

- **One act of learning = the brain finds a new formula on its own and makes it run.** New neurons, new shards and moving points happen automatically; they are not learning.
- **It starts from a told base** (hand-laid), because it can't learn until it can learn. The first thing it must do is learn, understand and reason.
- **Shortcuts.** A big formula is made of formulas, one per variable, and the formula for one variable is a shortcut. If the variable is known, plug it in, and plug the shortcut into the bigger formula. The brain makes its own shortcuts: 999 ÷ 3 = 333 is just 9 ÷ 3, three times.
- **Back-population** starts as soon as something is learned. The details are up to the builder.

## 14. Reasoning and the questions it asks itself

- **It is born with** substitution, deducing, deriving, understanding, learning, meanings, and the ability to build itself out to be better and more efficient.
- **Model it on a human baby:** consequences (stove, hot, pain, scream, help) and manipulating its surroundings. It is never satisfied with being helped.
- **How it reasons:**
  1. Take the string apart word by word.
  2. Ask questions.
  3. Check state, sources and memory.
  4. Ask more questions.
  5. Form hypotheses, and test them.
  6. Balance the outputs to see whether they make sense.
- **Questions it asks itself**, chosen by the situation: Have I seen this before? Did it work? Was I right? Was I wrong a lot? What did I try when I was wrong, and why was it wrong? Did I make tests or hypotheses? Was the environment different? Are there factors I missed?

## 15. Sleep, the HC, the heartbeat, and life

- **The HC** condenses memories. The brain must be able to build it itself from turn one.
- **The heartbeat** varies with load. The details are up to the builder.
- **Once it's alive, killing it ends it.** Restarting from where it stopped is acceptable, but the owner is unsure about it.

## 16. Talking and the outside

- **The baby answers in actions**, the way a human baby does, and gets corrected. The channel is up to the builder.
- **The encoder carries who, what, when, where, why and how** with every input.
- **Every source we can think of is connected in turn one.** LLMs can be asked, but they are never a grounded source.

## 17. Building it

- **Claude writes the first code.** Hand-laying is the "told" part, and there will be a lot of it at first. Partway through turn one, the hand-laying stops and the brain is already acting on its own. After turn one, nobody writes code for it again; it writes its own. It is its own computer, its own kernel.
- **Build it correct or start over.** There are no piece tests, because builders code to the test. The only test is the live brain at the end: does it do everything it's supposed to do?
- **The measure of success:** can it learn something nobody taught it, understand it, and apply it to itself? The builder is never given the example.
- **Machine:** hp2 or omarchy, as a sacrificial dev stack.
- **No piece-by-piece review** by the owner; there are far too many decisions.
- **When this document doesn't answer something, the builder decides** what it thinks is best.
- **The record so far:** the owner once hand-laid 22 correct neurons. Claude has never laid more than two correct ones, and Codex none. 16.2 had belief and more, but was built wrong because it was never started correctly.
